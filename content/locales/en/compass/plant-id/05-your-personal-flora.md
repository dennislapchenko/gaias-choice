## The same fifty steps

Go back to where this course started. The same door, the same direction, the
same fifty steps you walked the evening you picked plant #1. Walk them slowly,
and this time count out loud: every plant you can name, with the rung you
earned for it. If a child was along in chapter 1, bring them now — the
pointing hand will be the same, and the silence will not.

That count is the course's measurement. Not a quiz score, not a completed
checklist — a number of beings on your own street that were strangers a few
weeks ago and now have names, families, and paper trails. Chapter 1 opened
with an honest silence: a child points at the third plant in ten steps, and
most adults have nothing to say. The silence is what this course was built to
replace, and the walk is where you find out whether it worked.

One thing the walk will also show you: loose ends. A pressing that has been
sitting in a book since chapter 2. Three or four more from chapter 4, some
dry, some not. A life-list that grew line by line in the order you happened
to meet things, which is no order at all. Chapter 4 sent you here with its
question — at what point do loose pages become a flora, and what makes it
*yours*? This chapter is that point. There is nothing new to learn in it,
which is exactly the plan: a Summit assembles; it does not add.

## The promise

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- mount and label a herbarium sheet so that it reads back into its
  first-meeting record;
- turn the life-list into a flora — ordered by family, bounded to home
  ground, every plant carrying its earned rung;
- log a plant's seasonal change and read one plant across a whole year;
- name where the depth lives after this course lets go, and judge whether a
  key, a book, or a society is worth your winter;
- judge the finished flora against chapter 1's promise, in one dated sentence.

## The core

### Mounting day

The pressing you set in chapter 2 has been drying longer than any reading
pace requires — it is ready. Chapter 4's sheets follow at their own speed:
mount each one as it passes the dryness test, not before, and let the slow
ones take their weeks. Mounting itself is chapter 4's technique with the
weight lifted off: the specimen onto its sheet, strips holding it, nothing
rushed.

Then the label — and a retrieval pull before you write one. Chapter 4 gave
the label a name: it is the record's twin. Which two fields does the pocket
rule say no record survives missing? Say them, then check: place and date.
Every label is copied out of its plant's first-meeting record — name with
binomial, family, place, date, rung — so a sheet and a notebook page point at
each other forever. A mounted sheet without a label is a dead leaf on nice
paper; a labeled one is a record you can hold.

The collecting ethic stands over the finished flora exactly as it stood over
the picking: leave-more-than-you-take is not a gathering rule you retire at
the mounting table, it is the standing law of the whole artifact. Five sheets
of common plants is the herbarium's full size — the depth of a flora is never
measured in paper, and everything scarce or doubtful lives on as photographs.

### From life-list to flora

Here is chapter 4's seed, answered in full. Sheets pile up and records pile
up, and the pile becomes a flora at the moment it gains two things: **order**
and a **boundary**.

The order comes from chapter 2. Rewrite the life-list grouped by family —
mints together, composites together, the lone unplaced strangers at the end —
and give every line a certainty column with its rung from the ladder. Recall
the five rungs bottom to top before you draw the column; the notebook still
records the level you earned, never the one you want. Ordered this way, the
list starts doing work a diary cannot: gaps become visible (five composites
and not one mustard — your street has mustards, you just haven't looked), and
the family faces you learned become the shelf labels of your own collection.

The boundary is chapter 1's oldest idea: home ground. A flora is not a list
of every plant you ever met anywhere — it is the plants of the ~1 km you can
walk daily, known to the level you can defend. That is what makes it yours:
the boundary is your daily ground, and every rung in the column is one you
personally earned. Nobody else can own this document, and no app can generate
it.

And the flora stays alive by one standing habit: when a stranger appears on
home ground — and every spring sends one — it gets the full sequence. The
verdict workflow, an honest rung, a first-meeting record, a line in its
family's section. The course ends; the intake procedure does not.

### The same plant, a new season

Plant #1 — the plantain, for most readers — has now been visited three times:
met in chapter 1, re-identified and upgraded in chapter 4, and passed again
on today's walk. Look at it once more and write one dated line under its
record: what the season has done to it since you first knelt there. Taller
stalks, browner heads, a flatter rosette — whatever is true today.

One line looks like nothing. It is the deepest habit this course can leave
you with, because a plant is not a photograph — it is a being with four
faces a year, and a reader who has only seen the June face has met a quarter
of the plant. This is also why your own repeated observation counts as a live
source in the three-source rule: a stand watched across seasons is an
acquaintance, not a lookup.

### Where the depth lives

This course taught the vital fifth of identification: characters, six family
faces, one workflow, one record. The other four-fifths — the graminoids that
laugh at beginners, the small white crucifers, the mosses, the thousand
species your six families don't cover — you can now teach yourself, and these
are the three places it lives:

- **A real regional key.** The test before you buy: does it make you look at
  the plant, feature by feature, or at photographs? A dichotomous key for your
  own region is the characters, industrialized — a book of look-alike checks.
  A picture book teaches matching, and you already know from chapter 2 where
  matching fails.
- **A local flora.** A published account of what actually grows in your area,
  which quietly answers half of every look-alike check — a species that has
  never been recorded within a hundred kilometers is a poor first guess.
- **The nearest botanical society.** Field walks with people who know are
  live third sources by the dozen — and the fastest correction service your
  list will ever have. One walk with a sharp-eyed stranger is worth a winter
  of solitary keying.

### Ten known deeply

A last word about the count from the walk, before the practice makes you
take it. The count matters as a before-and-after; it must not become a
score. A life-list can quietly turn into a trophy cabinet — more lines, more
species, a number to grow — and at that moment the flora stops measuring
what it was built to measure. Ten plants known deeply — watched through
seasons, sketched, keyed, revisited — beat a thousand logged, because the
list was never the point. Relationship was. The plantain you have now met in
two seasons is worth more than fifty names harvested from an app in an
afternoon, and the difference is the entire course.

## Practice

The walk outdoors, the assembly at the table — 40–60 minutes all told, and
the walk goes first while the light lasts.

1. **Walk the same fifty steps** as chapter 1 — same door, same direction.
   Count aloud every plant you can name and the rung it holds. Write the
   count in the notebook, dated.
2. **Visit plant #1** on the way and write its seasonal line — one dated
   sentence on what the season has done to it since chapter 1.
3. **Mount the chapter-2 pressing** and every chapter-4 sheet that passes
   the dryness test; leave the damp ones under weight for another week.
4. **Label each mounted sheet from its record** — binomial, family, place,
   date, rung. No label leaves a field blank that the record can fill.
5. **Rewrite the life-list as the flora**: grouped by family, a certainty
   column, the unplaced strangers gathered at the end as the to-do list they
   are.
6. **Copy or print the crib** into the notebook's front cover.
7. **Judge the flora against chapter 1's promise** — a named world on your
   own street — and write the verdict under today's count: one honest, dated
   sentence.

**How to know it worked:** the notebook holds a dated count and a dated
verdict, at least one sheet is mounted and labeled back to its record, and a
stranger could open the list and see your street organized by family — with
an honest rung beside every name.

**Short on time:** the fifty-step walk and the count — mounting can wait for
a rainy afternoon; the count cannot, because it is the measurement.

## Next

There is no chapter 6, and the flora does not need one. What comes next is
seasons.

The depth lives where the core said: a real regional key for your own
country, a local flora, and the nearest botanical society's field walks —
one walk a season would outgrow this course within a year. The plantain's
wheel wants its remaining lines; the unplaced strangers at the end of the
list are your winter keying work; and the crib inside the cover keeps the
vocabulary while the practice keeps the skill.

And when a confirmed name finally raises the other question — *what is this
plant for?* — that question leaves this course by the door it was always
going to: [the herbalism course](/compass/herbalism/01-the-whole-plant),
where use lives behind its own safety frame, red flags first.

The named world was on your street the whole time. Now it answers when a
child points.
