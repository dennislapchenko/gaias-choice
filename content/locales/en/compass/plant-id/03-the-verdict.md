## Strangers with name tags

Open the life-list chapter 1 started. Look at the rung column, honestly.
Plant #1 stands at "likely" — earned, character by character, at your own
table. Plant #2, read through chapter 2's characters, likewise. And then the
other lines: the plant an app named on a walk, the one a friend named in
passing, the one you "just know" because everyone knows it. Rung: guess.
Guess. Guess.

A list of guesses is not a flora. It is a list of strangers wearing name
tags — and chapter 2 showed you, on its umbel page, exactly what a name tag
can cost. That chapter left one question standing, and it is this chapter's
whole job: *two white umbels wear the same face — one is folklore-food, one
kills. How does anyone honestly move past "likely" with twins like that?*

Not by looking harder at the same photo. Not by asking the app twice. The
answer is a procedure — the same five moves, in the same order, every time a
name matters — and by the end of this chapter you will have run it on a real
plant from your own list. The procedure has a conscience built in, and the
conscience is the part that answers the twins question.

## The promise

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- run **the verdict workflow** — five steps, fixed order — on any plant whose
  name you need to trust;
- run **the look-alike check** and write the exclusions down, not just feel
  them;
- apply the three-source rule in full: two written sources checked feature by
  feature, plus one live confirmation;
- give an app's confidence score the same scrutiny as internet folklore — and
  still get more out of the app than you did before this course;
- photograph the parts a key actually reads, instead of the pretty bloom;
- accept "keyed, not confirmed — stay away" as a successful verdict.

## The core

### The verdict workflow

Here is the instrument, whole, before we take it apart:

1. **Family gestalt first.** Name the family before you name the plant.
2. **Characters against a real key** or field guide, feature by feature.
3. **The app** — as one more hypothesis, its confidence score given the same
   skepticism as internet folklore.
4. **The look-alike check** (mandatory): what else could this be, and how do
   I exclude it?
5. **Three independent sources close it** — the three-source rule.

The order is the method, and each position has a reason. A retrieval pull
from chapter 2 before we walk it: why does **family gestalt** come before
any species name — what does calling the family actually buy? Say it, then
check: a family is a face, and the face narrows a thousand candidates to a
dozen before you open a single book. Step 2 is where **the characters** from
chapter 2 stop being observation and start being evidence: a real key or a
proper field guide asks them one by one — arrangement, margin, stem section,
flower parts — and you answer from the plant in front of you, not from
memory. Step 3 places the app exactly where chapter 1 promised it would
live; step 4 is the conscience; step 5 closes the door. Notice what the
workflow does not contain: a taste step. There is none, at any rung, for any
plant. Smell is a character — crush a leaf and read it. Taste is not an
identification tool; it is the mistake the whole ladder exists to prevent.

Two frozen lines from chapter 1 hold with full force here: the gestalt alone
never earns more than "likely", and an app alone never earns more than
"guess". The workflow does not soften those limits — it is built out of
them.

### The look-alike check

Step 4 is the answer to chapter 2's question, so give it the space it has
earned. The look-alike check asks, in writing: **what else could this be,
and how do I exclude it?** Not "does my plant match the picture" — the
deadly question's twin — but its inversion: *which other plant also matches,
and which character separates them?* You are not done identifying when your
plant fits a description. You are done when everything else has stopped
fitting.

This is how anyone honestly moves past "likely" with twins in the meadow:
you name the twin out loud, you find the character that splits the pair, and
you check that character on the plant in front of you. The twins problem is
not solved by confidence. It is solved by exclusion, written down — and then
step 5 makes even your exclusion answer to two books and a living witness.

| The pair | The shared face | The character that splits them | The stakes |
|---|---|---|---|
| cow parsley (*Anthriscus sylvestris*) · poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*) | white double umbrella, feathery leaves, road and meadow edges | hemlock's stem is smooth with purple blotches; cow parsley's is ridged, furrowed, green | the temperate world's deadliest confusion |
| comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) · foxglove first-year rosette (*Digitalis purpurea*) | a big basal rosette of broad, veined leaves, no flower in sight | comfrey is rough with bristly hairs; foxglove is softly downy with a finely toothed margin | a real, documented, repeatedly fatal mix-up |
| stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*) · white deadnettle (*Lamium album*) | the nettle costume — toothed, nettle-shaped leaves | deadnettle's square stem and two-lipped white flowers; no stinging hairs | none — the harmless pair to train on |
| yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) · white umbels | feathery leaves, flat white flower head, the same meadow | yarrow's head is a composite — tiny ray-and-disc florets, no double umbrella | the herbalism course warns of exactly this pair |

That last row is an old warning meeting you again: [the herbalism course's
harvest chapter](/compass/herbalism/08-the-harvest-year) flags yarrow's
white-umbel neighbors as the one place its kitchen ten touches real danger.
Two courses, one lesson, because the meadow only teaches it one way.

### Three sources close it — and symmetric skepticism

The three-source rule was born in [herbalism's harvest
chapter](/compass/herbalism/08-the-harvest-year) as a gate before any
gathering. This course inherits it as the top of the ladder, and here is its
full training. **Two written sources** — a regional key, a photographed
field guide, a botanical description — each checked *feature by feature*
against the plant, not skimmed for a matching picture. Plus **one live
confirmation**: a person who genuinely knows the plant standing beside you,
or your own repeated observation of that exact stand across a season. The
rule works because the three fail independently — a bad photo match, a wrong
assumption, and an over-confident friend rarely all land on the same wrong
plant.

Now the app, plainly, because it deserves plain words in both directions.
Identification apps are genuinely good and getting better — they put a
competent first hypothesis in your pocket, and a reader who knows the
characters gets *more* from an app, not less: you can interrogate its
suggestion instead of swallowing it. And their failure modes are just as
real: grasses and sedges, juvenile plants, basal rosettes without flowers,
and regional twins the training data never separated — which is precisely
the list of cases where a mistake costs most. So the rule of **symmetric
skepticism**: the app's "98% confident" and a stranger's forum post get
identical scrutiny. Recall from chapter 1 what **app-as-hypothesis**
committed you to — say it before reading on: an app proposes, never rules.
"The app was sure" is not a source. It is one voice, and it does not get a
casting vote.

One small note that will save you years of confusion: the record you keep
should carry the **Latin binomial** alongside the folk name. Folk names
collide — a dozen plants answer to "wild carrot" somewhere; *Daucus carota*
answers to exactly one. The binomial is the one unambiguous handle; the folk
name is the relationship layer. Keep both, and let the binomial do the legal
work.

And the boundary, restated where it matters most: "confirmed" is the only
rung from which a use question may even be *asked* — and answering it still
belongs to [the herbalism course's safety
frame](/compass/herbalism/07-household-protocols), not to this one. This
course ends at the name.

### Photograph the parts a key reads

Most plant photos are portraits — the bloom, centered, in good light. A key
cannot use a portrait. When you photograph a plant for later keying, shoot
the evidence:

- [ ] the whole plant with its habitat behind it — habitat is a character;
- [ ] the leaf arrangement on the stem — opposite, alternate, rosette;
- [ ] one leaf laid flat, margin in sharp focus;
- [ ] the stem up close — ridges, hairs, blotches, and the cross-section
      shape your fingers read;
- [ ] the flower close enough to count parts — and one shot from the side;
- [ ] a leaf's underside — hairs and veins live there.

Six frames, one minute. The pretty bloom can be frame seven, for love rather
than evidence.

## Worked example

Three plants, one workflow, three different honest endings. Run them with
me.

**The easy case: garden mint.** A pot on a step, or a patch by a fence.
Step 1 — the face: square stem between your fingers, leaves in opposite
pairs, a sharp clean smell when a leaf is crushed. The mints, called in ten
seconds. Step 2 — the key asks its questions: toothed margin, yes; flowers
in whorls at the nodes, yes; the aroma unmistakably mint rather than
deadnettle's green nothing. Step 3 — the app agrees at high confidence,
which is now one more voice, not a verdict. Step 4 — what else could this
be? Within the mints, a handful of cousins; the smell and the flower
arrangement exclude the deadnettles outright, and for a garden plant the
stakes are a mislabeled pot, not a hospital. Step 5 — two field guides
agree feature by feature, and the person who planted the pot confirms.
Three sources, trivially. **Verdict: confirmed.** Some verdicts are cheap.
The workflow costs five minutes and gives you the same certainty it gives
the hard cases — that is what makes it a habit you can afford.

**The hard case: a white roadside umbel.** Summer verge, waist-high, lacy
white double umbrellas — chapter 2's warning standing in the sun. Step 1:
the umbels, instantly — an umbrella made of umbrellas. Which is exactly the
problem, because that face is both the family's giveaway and its disguise.
Step 2, against a real key: leaves finely divided, stem hollow — and here
the key demands the stem's surface. Ridged and green says cow parsley.
Smooth with purple blotches says poison hemlock, and hemlock has stood on a
thousand verges wearing cow-parsley's face. Step 3: the app says cow
parsley, 91%. Noted as one hypothesis. Step 4, in writing: *could this be
hemlock?* The stem on this plant is furrowed and green, no blotches; the
crushed leaf smells green and carroty rather than foul and mousy; the
habitat fits both, so habitat excludes nothing. Keyed, with the twin
named and the splitting characters checked. Step 5: two written sources
agree — but no person who knows this plant has stood beside you, and you
have watched this stand for one afternoon, not a season. **Verdict: keyed,
not confirmed — and for this family, that spells: do not touch.** Write
exactly that in the list. This is the workflow succeeding, not failing —
the honest output of a verdict is sometimes distance. Beginners never
forage this family, full stop; no rung changes that, and nothing in this
paragraph is an invitation.

**The app-misled case: a basal rosette.** Waste ground in early spring: a
handsome rosette of broad, veined leaves, no flower to help. The app says
comfrey — high confidence, and comfrey is a beloved name, so the mind wants
to agree. Run the workflow anyway. Step 1: without a flower, the gestalt is
weak — rosettes are half the world in March. Step 2, characters in hand:
comfrey's leaves should be rough, almost bristly, hairs you can rasp with a
thumb; these are softly downy. The margin here is finely toothed; the veins
do not run down into winged stalks the way comfrey's do. The characters are
voting against the app. Step 4 — what else could this be? The look-alike
table answers: a foxglove's first-year rosette, *Digitalis purpurea* — a
real and documented deadly confusion, made exactly this way, by people who
trusted a leaf-shape and a confident name. The soft down and toothed margin
fit foxglove first. **Verdict: guess overturned — likely foxglove, keyed
only when the flower spike settles it; either way, hands off.** The app
opened the question. It does not get to close it — and this case is why
that sentence is this chapter's rule and not a slogan.

## Practice

Outdoors, to a plant you can stand in front of — 30–60 minutes, one field
guide or key in hand. A child along is an asset here: nothing slows you to
key-reading speed like answering "why?" at every character.

1. Pick **one plant from your life-list that stands at "guess"** — the
   app-named one, the friend-named one. Go to it.
2. **Step 1, aloud:** call the family from its face. If one of chapter 2's
   six fits, name it and say which giveaways you see.
3. **Step 2, in writing:** work the key or guide character by character —
   arrangement, margin, stem section, flower parts, smell (crush a leaf;
   never taste). Note each answer next to the character.
4. **Step 3:** ask the app. Write its proposal and score down as one
   hypothesis line — no more weight than that.
5. **Step 4, the conscience:** write "what else could this be?" and answer
   it with at least one named look-alike, plus the character that excludes
   it — or the honest note that you cannot exclude it today.
6. **Step 5:** name your two written sources. Then the live source: a person
   who knows, or the start of your own season-long watch of this stand. If
   all three line up, the rung is confirmed. If not, log the rung the plant
   actually earned.
7. Update the plant's life-list line: new rung, date, one-line verdict.

**How to know it worked:** the plant's line now carries a rung with a
written trail behind it — sources named, look-alikes excluded on paper — and
you would hand that page to the strictest person you know. An honest "keyed,
not confirmed — leaving it alone" passes this test in full.

**Short on time:** run only steps 1, 2, and 4 — gestalt, characters,
look-alike check — on one plant, and log the rung it earns. Fifteen minutes,
and one name tag becomes evidence.

## Now you can

> **"An app opens the question; three sources close it."**

- Run the five-step verdict workflow on any plant whose name has to be
  trusted, in order, without skipping the conscience.
- Write a look-alike check that names the twin and the character that
  excludes it — or honestly records that it can't be excluded today.
- Close a verdict with two written sources checked feature by feature plus
  one live confirmation — and refuse to close it with fewer.
- Give an app's confidence score exactly the scrutiny you give a forum
  post, while getting more from the app than you did before.
- Photograph the six frames a key can actually read.
- Accept distance as a verdict: "keyed, not confirmed — stay away" is the
  workflow working.
