## The third plant in ten steps

Imagine the walk — or remember it, if you have a small child, because it
happens on every walk. Ten steps from the door, a hand points down for the
third time: "what's that?" Not the dog, not the car. The green thing by the
fence. And the honest answer, for most adults on their own street, is
silence — or "some weed," which is silence wearing clothes.

Sit with how strange that is. You can name every app on your phone and
forty brands of car, and you walk daily past several hundred living species
whose names you have never once needed — until a child needed them from
you. The child is not being difficult. Wonder is a developmental need, the
way food is; a parent who can answer with a real name feeds it, and a
parent who says "some weed" teaches, gently and repeatedly, that the green
world is background. Children also happen to be the best identification
companions on Earth: they move at plant speed, they crouch without being
asked, and they will not let you walk past the third plant in ten steps.
You do not need a child for this course — but if one lives with you, you
already own the equipment.

This course exists to end the silence — not everywhere, just where you
live. Five chapters from now, the same street will answer back. This first
chapter hands you the whole territory in miniature: the artifact you will
build, the honesty instrument the whole craft stands on, and your first
plant — met, named, and written down tonight.

## The promise

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- start **the personal flora** — the notebook, the life-list, and the plan

### The personal flora

A *flora*, in the botanist's sense, is a book that describes the plants of
one defined place. Yours will be handmade and small, and it lives in three
physical parts: a **bound field notebook** (any notebook — there is nothing
to buy), a **life-list** (one line per plant met, each line carrying the
certainty level you earned for it), and, by the course's end, a **starter
herbarium of five mounted sheets**. That whole kit is **the personal
flora** — the artifact every chapter of this course adds a piece to.

The defined place matters as much as the book. Your flora covers **home
ground**: roughly the one kilometer you can walk daily — the street, the
lane, the field edge, the park. Not the national flora's ten thousand
species; the two or three hundred that are actually your neighbors. This
is not a beginner's training-wheels compromise. It is the point. Stephen
Harrod Buhner spent his life arguing that plants are living beings you
meet, not data you log — and meeting begins with telling beings apart.
Naming is the precondition of respect: you cannot honestly claim a
relationship with a being you could not tell from its deadly twin. A
thousand plants vaguely recognized is an acquaintance with none of them.
One kilometer, truly known, is a relationship — and it is also, quietly,
the only scale at which the rest of this course's method physically works,
because you will be returning to the same plants across seasons.

### The certainty ladder

Now the honesty instrument. Almost everyone who "knows plants" a little is
carrying a pile of names they never checked — from a grandmother, a
half-remembered book, an app. Some of those names are right. The problem
is that you cannot say *which*, and in a world with deadly look-alikes
that is not a small problem. Peter Ralston's discipline applies here
exactly: not-knowing is the honest starting point, and the fix is not to
know less — it is to state precisely how much you know. The ladder makes
that practical. Five rungs, bottom to top, each naming what promotes a
plant to it:

1. **stranger** — noticed, not named. (Promotion: propose any name.)
2. **guess** — a name proposed, by you or an app, unverified. (Promotion:
   call the family from gestalt + characters that agree.)
3. **likely** — family called, a few characters agree, look-alikes not yet
   excluded. (Promotion: work a real key or field guide character by
   character AND run the look-alike check.)
4. **keyed** — keyed feature by feature, look-alikes considered and
   excluded. (Promotion: three independent sources agree — two written +
   one live.)
5. **confirmed** — the three-source rule satisfied. The only rung from
   which a use question may even be ASKED — and answering it still belongs
   to the herbalism course.

Some words in those promotions — *gestalt*, *characters*, *the look-alike
check*, *the three-source rule* — are the next two chapters' whole
business; the ladder shows you the territory before you walk it. Two lines
to memorize now, because the whole course leans on them: **the gestalt
alone never earns more than "likely". An app alone never earns more than
"guess".**

The ladder is a recording discipline before it is anything else: the
notebook records the level you *earned*, never the one you want. A
life-list full of plants parked at "guess" is not an embarrassment — it is
an accurate map of your knowledge, which is worth more than a flattering
one. For quick reference:

| Rung | What it means | What promotes a plant to the next rung |
|---|---|---|
| **stranger** | noticed, not named | propose any name |
| **guess** | a name proposed, by you or an app, unverified | call the family from gestalt + characters that agree |
| **likely** | family called, a few characters agree, look-alikes not yet excluded | work a real key or field guide character by character AND run the look-alike check |
| **keyed** | keyed feature by feature, look-alikes considered and excluded | three independent sources agree — two written + one live |
| **confirmed** | the three-source rule satisfied | — the ladder's top; only here may a use question even be asked |

### App-as-hypothesis

You almost certainly have an identification app, and this course wants you
to keep it. The apps are genuinely good and getting better — pointing one
at a flower and getting a plausible Latin name in two seconds is a small
miracle, and it would be dishonest to sneer at it. The discipline is one
sentence: **an app proposes, never rules.** Its output is a hypothesis —
the top of a ranked list of guesses — and on the ladder a hypothesis lands
exactly where the ladder says: *guess*. Not because the app is stupid, but
because a confidence percentage is the app grading its own homework, and
nobody gets to do that here. Call the discipline **app-as-hypothesis**.
Chapter 3 gives the app its proper seat in the verdict workflow — a
respected witness, never the judge — and names the honest places where
apps still stumble. Until then, one habit: every name an app gives you
gets written down with a question mark and the rung "guess". You will be
surprised how different that feels from believing it.

### Where a name ends

Now the boundary, stated once, plainly, before anything else in this
course happens — because identification is a skill people reach for with
dinner on their minds, and that path has bodies on it.

**Identified is not usable.** This course teaches you to know what stands
in front of you — that is its whole scope, beginning to end. It teaches
nothing about eating, brewing, or applying any plant, and a correct name
is not a permission slip: some of the most confident foraging deaths on
record began with a name that was *almost* right, because the deadly twins
of the plant world wear their edible cousins' faces well enough to fool a
person who stopped checking. That is why the ladder exists, why its top
rung demands three independent sources, and why even "confirmed" only
earns the *right to ask* a use question. Answering it belongs to a
different discipline with its own safety frame — on this site, [the
herbalism course](/compass/herbalism/07-household-protocols), whose
red-flags protocol governs anything that goes near a body, and whose
[harvest chapter](/compass/herbalism/08-the-harvest-year) is where the
three-source rule was born. If you came here from that course, welcome
back — this one builds the identification half it told you to go earn.

## Worked example

Plant #1 for this course is chosen for you, because it is almost certainly
within fifty steps of your door: **ribwort plantain** (*Plantago
lanceolata*) — the narrow-leaved rosette that grows through every lawn
edge, path side, and trampled verge in the temperate world. Here is one
evening at the kitchen table, walking it up the ladder.

It starts as a **stranger**: the tough little rosette by the gate you have
stepped over a thousand times. You bring one leaf and one flower stalk
inside. A name gets proposed — maybe from memory, maybe an app says
*Plantago lanceolata* at some confident percentage, and if you took the
herbalism course you may already "know" this plant, because it sits in
that course's kitchen ten. Fine. All three of those are the same thing:
a proposal. Rung: **guess** — and notice the small honesty shock if you
"knew" it: knowing-without-checking was a guess all along; the ladder just
makes you say so.

Now check what agrees. The leaves stand in a flat basal rosette. Each leaf
is long and narrow with three to five veins running parallel the whole
length — not branching like a net, which is unusual and distinctive. The
flower stalk is leafless, deeply grooved, and carries a single dense,
dark, cone-like head with a halo of pale stamens. Every one of those
observations agrees with the proposed name, and together they call a
family (the plantains). Rung: **likely** — earned in maybe twenty minutes.

And there it stops tonight, honestly. You have not worked a key character
by character; you have not asked what else wears this face (there are
other plantains — a greater one, a hoary one) or excluded them. That is
chapter 3's craft. So the life-list's first line reads something like:

> 1 · ribwort plantain (*Plantago lanceolata*?) · **likely** · lawn edge
> by the gate · 24 Aug

One evening, one plant, one honest rung — and the named world has begun,
one line long.

## Practice

Outdoors and then at the table — 30–50 minutes total. Take the child if
one lives with you; they are better at step 2 than you are.

1. Walk **fifty steps from your door** — no farther. Any direction.
2. Pick **one plant** that catches you. Not the prettiest — the one you
   have walked past most often. If ribwort plantain is there, it makes a
   friendly first plant; any plant works.
3. Spend ten unhurried minutes with it. Look at where and how it grows,
   the leaves, the stem, any flower. Take one leaf (and a flower if there
   are many) or a few close photos.
4. Tonight, at the table: propose a name — memory, app, or a field guide
   if one lives on your shelf. Write the name down with a question mark.
   The plant now stands at **guess**.
5. List what you can check that agrees or disagrees — leaf shape, veins,
   how the leaves are arranged, the flower's build. If a few real
   observations agree and you can name the family, the plant stands at
   **likely**. If you can't — it honestly stands at guess, and that is a
   correct result, not a failure.
6. Open a bound notebook — any notebook, there is nothing to buy — and
   write **life-list line #1**: number, name (with its question mark if it
   keeps one), the rung, where it grows, today's date.

**How to know it worked:** life-list line #1 exists, and the rung on it
has reasons attached — you can say out loud what you checked and what you
didn't, and the rung matches.

**Short on time:** one plant, one honest rung, one line. Ten minutes at
the window with the nearest green thing still opens the flora.
