## The margin test

One question, and be honest with yourself before you look anywhere: **what
did plant #1's leaf margin look like?** The ribwort plantain you worked at
your own table in chapter 1 — toothed, smooth, wavy? While you're at it: how
many veins did you count? What was growing next to it?

You met that plant weeks ago. You earned its rung yourself, character by
character, and it was real work honestly done. And you almost certainly
cannot answer the margin question now without walking back and looking —
which is not a failure of your memory but a fact about all memory. Chapter 3
closed on exactly this: *the workflow hands you a verdict today — but next
spring you won't remember it. Where does a verdict live so it survives your
forgetting?*

Here is the answer, and it is this chapter's whole cargo: a verdict lives in
**the first-meeting record** — a fixed set of fields written at the meeting,
while the plant is in front of you and the evidence is checkable. Memory is
where verdicts go to dissolve. Paper is where they keep.

## The promise

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- write a **first-meeting record** — ten fields, no gaps that matter — for
  any plant you have worked to a rung;
- decide go/no-go on collecting from any stand, with legality and ethics
  ruling before technique ever enters;
- press a specimen properly and build a herbarium sheet whose label is the
  record's twin;
- rerun the verdict workflow on a plant you "already know" — the return —
  and upgrade its thin old line into a full record;
- let digital photos index the paper record instead of replacing it.

## The core

### The first-meeting record

The record is ten fields, and the order is part of the discipline:

| Field | What goes there |
|---|---|
| **Place** | where exactly — named precisely enough to return to the same stand |
| **Habitat** | what kind of ground: verge, meadow, wall foot, shade or sun, wet or dry |
| **Date** | the day of the meeting — season is half of what a plant looks like |
| **Characters observed** | what your fingers and eyes actually read: arrangement, margin, stem section, flower parts, smell |
| **Sketch** | one leaf or one flower, drawn from the plant |
| **Family** | the face you called, from the gestalt and its giveaways |
| **Name with binomial** | folk name and Latin binomial together |
| **Certainty level** | the rung earned — never the rung wished for |
| **Look-alikes considered** | the twins named, and the character that excluded each |
| **Open questions** | what you could not settle today — the honest tail |

Read the first three fields again, because they carry the chapter's pocket
rule before it is spoken: **place and date are the two fields that make a
record a record.** A description without a place cannot be returned to; an
observation without a date cannot be compared to next spring. Everything
else can be thin on a bad day. Those two cannot be absent.

Two of the fields are old friends — and that is the point. Recall from
chapter 2 why **sketching as seeing** earns a field that a photograph
doesn't fill — try to say it before reading on: drawing a leaf forces you to
actually look, and the bad drawing that results is an instrument, not art.
And recall **the look-alike check** from chapter 3 — its question, word for
word, before you read on: *what else could this be, and how do I exclude
it?* The record's ninth field is that check, preserved. A verdict whose
exclusions are written down can be re-argued next year; a verdict without
them has to be taken on faith from a person who no longer remembers — you.

One placement note for the digital age: photographs are wonderful, and the
six keying frames from chapter 3 belong with every record. But the photos
are the **index, not the record** — they point at the paper, they do not
replace it. A camera roll is a thousand unlabeled meetings; ten written
fields are one meeting kept. Note the photo dates in the record and let the
phone serve the notebook, not the other way around.

### Leave-more-than-you-take

The next section will teach you to press plants — which means this section
must come first, because collecting is an act with rules that outrank
technique. And your **home ground** is not exempt because it is familiar —
the kilometer you walk daily is exactly where these rules do their work.
Call the whole ethic **leave-more-than-you-take**, and let the law lead:

- **Protected species are never collected.** Not one leaf, not for any
  record. Learn your region's protected list — every country keeps one.
- **Protected areas have their own rules** — in reserves and national parks,
  collecting anything is typically forbidden. The camera is legal
  everywhere.
- **Private land needs permission.** A field margin belongs to someone.
- **Rarities are never collected, protected or not.** If a stand is small,
  struggling, or the only one you know — a photograph is the only honest
  specimen.
- **One where many grow.** Where a plant is genuinely abundant, take a
  single specimen, once — a leaf and a flower, not the plant's future.
- **When doubt or scarcity says don't pick — photograph.** The record loses
  nothing; the stand keeps everything.

| The stand in front of you | Collect? | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| protected species, anywhere | never | photograph, record, admire |
| inside a reserve or national park | no | photograph — it's what the rules are for |
| private land, no permission asked | no | ask, or photograph from the path |
| few individuals, struggling, or the only stand you know | no | photograph and note "scarce" in the record |
| identity below "keyed" on your own ladder | no | the camera collects doubt better than the press does |
| common plant, abundant stand, lawful ground | yes — one specimen | take the least the sheet needs; leave the root |

And name the tension honestly, because it is real: a herbarium takes life,
and this course otherwise teaches you to leave every stand stronger. The
two do sit at odds, and pretending otherwise would be the only dishonest
line in this course. Our answer is proportion: the starter herbarium is
**five sheets, common plants only, one specimen where many grow** — tiny on
purpose — and photography carries everything scarce, doubtful, or merely
beautiful. If even five feels like too many on your ground, press garden
plants and let the wild ones sit entirely in ink and pixels; the flora
loses nothing. Readers who came here from herbalism will recognize this
ethic as kin to [the harvest chapter's reciprocity
rules](/compass/herbalism/08-the-harvest-year) — same posture, smaller
take.

### Pressing and the herbarium sheet

Now, and only now, the technique — and it is honestly simple. A pressing
wants three things: **flat, dry, patient.** Lay the specimen between two
sheets of absorbent paper — newspaper is the classic — arrange it the way
you want to meet it forever (one leaf turned underside-up on purpose; the
underside is evidence), and put real weight on top: a stack of heavy books
does everything a bought press does. Change the damp paper after the first
day or two, then leave it alone. Thin leaves dry in a couple of weeks;
anything fleshy takes longer. The test is the same crispness herbalists
use: a specimen that bends instead of rustling is not done.

Write the label **at pressing time, not at mounting time** — a slip of
paper straight into the press beside the specimen, carrying place, date,
name with binomial, and certainty level. Weeks from now, four dried sheets
look alike; four labeled slips never do.

The mounting itself waits for chapter 5, when your first pressing — the one
chapter 2 addressed to the future — is fully dry. What matters today is the
sheet's grammar: specimen held by paper strips (never glued flat — a strip
lets a specimen be lifted and examined; glue makes every decision final),
and the label bottom-right, which is the first-meeting record compressed
onto one card. A sheet without a label is not a specimen; it is a dead leaf
with good posture.

## Worked example

The return, run on the page. Here is the shape of a chapter-1 life-list
line — thin, honest, and exactly what that evening could produce:

> *ribwort plantain — likely — rosette, parallel veins, leafless stalk,
> dense head.*

A real rung, really earned. Also: no place, no date, no look-alikes, no
binomial, and a margin you provably cannot recall. Now walk back to the
plant — same spot, fifty steps from your door — and run the full toolkit on
it: the characters read fresh, the verdict workflow end to end, the look-
alike check in writing. The upgraded page reads like this:

> **Place:** path edge by the gate, fifty steps out, east side.
> **Habitat:** trampled dry ground, full sun, mowed verge.
> **Date:** today's — late August.
> **Characters:** basal rosette; leaves lance-shaped, 3–5 strong parallel
> veins; margin near-entire, faintly toothed toward the tip; flower stalk
> leafless, deeply furrowed; head a dense dark cylinder with a pale ring of
> anthers. Crushed leaf: green, faintly bitter smell. No taste taken — the
> workflow has no taste step.
> **Sketch:** one leaf, veins counted; worse than last time, and clearer.
> **Family:** the plantains (Plantaginaceae).
> **Name:** ribwort plantain — *Plantago lanceolata*.
> **Certainty:** keyed — two written sources agree feature by feature;
> the live source is this stand itself, watched from here on, so
> "confirmed" arrives with the season, or with one knowledgeable friend.
> **Look-alikes considered:** greater plantain (*P. major* — broad oval
> leaves, excluded on leaf shape); hoary plantain (*P. media* — downy grey
> leaves, excluded on hairiness).
> **Open questions:** does the anther ring climb the head as it flowers?
> Check next visit — and note what winter does to the rosette.

Look at what the return just did that no new stranger could. The rung
climbed one honest step — likely to keyed — because you now own tools
chapter 1 hadn't given you. The margin question has an answer on paper,
forever. And the open-questions field has quietly become an appointment
with chapter 5: this same plant, one season later, one line about what
changed. That is the record working as a hinge — backward to the meeting,
forward to the next one.

## Practice

The return plus the pressing stack — 40–60 minutes, most of it outdoors.
Bring the notebook, the field guide, and a folder or big book for carrying
specimens flat.

1. **Walk back to plant #1** — the plantain from chapter 1, at its own spot.
2. **Rerun it fresh:** read the characters aloud as if it were a stranger,
   then take the verdict workflow end to end — gestalt, key, app if you
   like (one hypothesis), look-alike check in writing, sources named.
3. **Write its full first-meeting record** — all ten fields, place and date
   first. Leave nothing blank; "not settled today" is a real entry for the
   open-questions field.
4. **Choose 3–4 pressing candidates:** common plants only, confidently
   named — at "keyed" or better on your own ladder — from abundant stands
   on lawful ground. Run each through the go/no-go table before touching
   it. Any "no" means the camera collects it instead.
5. **Take the least the sheet needs** — a leaf and a flower, one specimen
   where many grow, roots left in the ground.
6. **Press the same day:** flat between absorbent paper, under real weight,
   one leaf underside-up per specimen. Write each label slip **now** —
   place, date, name with binomial, certainty — and press it beside its
   plant.
7. Add a line to the life-list for anything new the walk produced, at
   whatever rung it honestly earned.

**How to know it worked:** plant #1's page holds all ten fields — a record
you could hand to a stranger who could then find the stand, check the
verdict, and know exactly how sure you were — and a pressing stack sits
under weight in your home, every specimen already labeled, addressed to
chapter 5.

**Short on time:** upgrade plant #1's record from memory plus one photograph
— place and date exact, the rest as honest as memory allows, gaps marked as
gaps. Press nothing today; the stack can wait for the weekend.

## Now you can

> **"No place, no date — no record."**

- Write a ten-field first-meeting record at the meeting, while the evidence
  is still in front of you.
- Refuse a collection the go/no-go table refuses — and photograph instead,
  losing nothing from the record.
- Press a specimen flat, dry, and patient, with the label written at
  pressing time, not after.
- Rerun the verdict workflow on a plant you "already know" and watch what
  the return teaches that a new stranger can't.
- Keep the camera roll as the paper's index — pointing at records, never
  standing in for them.
