## One word is half a wall

What keeps a wall warm — and what keeps it dry? Sit with that for a moment, because if your answer is one word, *insulation*, half the wall is missing — and it is the half that decides whether the house is still healthy in twenty years.

Here is the gap. Walls in cold climates rarely fail by being cold; they fail by being *wet*. Warm indoor air carries water vapor — every kettle, shower and sleeping body loads it — and that vapor pushes outward through any wall it can enter. Somewhere inside a cold-climate wall in February there is a plane cold enough for that vapor to condense back into liquid. If the wall traps what condenses, the insulation slowly soaks (wet insulation insulates roughly like a wet sweater warms), the timber sits damp, and — recall **the mold triangle** from chapter 1; name its three legs and the one you control before reading on — the wall itself becomes the moisture leg you were supposed to be controlling.

Chapter 3 planted this exact debt and it is due today: *the frame you shortlisted is an empty skeleton — what fills and wraps it without turning the wall into a raincoat?* The answer is a rule, three fillings, two plasters, and a coat of a very particular cut.

## The core

### The wool coat rule

Two coats, one lesson. A **raincoat** is sealed: nothing in, and — the fatal half — nothing out. Seal a wall on both faces and any water that enters (and it enters: one torn seam, one nail hole, one winter of vapor push) has no way to leave. It stays, it accumulates, and the mold triangle gets its moisture leg handed over permanently. A **wool coat** works the opposite way: it is windproof enough outside to stop moving air from stealing your heat, and open enough *through its whole thickness* that moisture passes outward and leaves.

So the rule, born here and carried to the end of the course: **windproof outside, vapor-open through — a wall is a wool coat, never a raincoat.** The engineering behind it fits in one sentence: build the layers so each one is more open to vapor than the one behind it, and the wall dries outward faster than it can ever wet. Windproof is not the same as vapor-tight — a layer can stop moving air yet let vapor diffuse through, and that combination is precisely what the outer face wants. (How the *whole house* handles its air — and why airtight and vapor-open turn out to be allies, not enemies — is chapter 7's territory; today we dress one wall.)

### The filling: wood-fiber, hemp, straw-clay

Three natural fillings earn a place in this climate, and none of them is a miracle. The table is the instrument — it feeds directly into the worked example and the walk.

| Material | What it is | Warmth, honestly | Moisture behavior | Where it honestly loses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Wood-fiber** (batts + boards) | forest industry's offcuts, fluffed and pressed | good insulation per centimeter; the boards double as the wind layer | excellent buffer — absorbs vapor surges and releases them without harm | real money; a factory product — ask what binds it, not just what grows it |
| **Hemp** (batts) | hemp fiber, usually with a binder fiber | same league as wood-fiber | very good buffer, naturally rot-resistant | young supply chains, patchy availability — and the binder is often polyester: run the five-year test on the *binder*, not the plant |
| **Straw-clay** (light in-fill) | loose straw tumbled in clay slip, packed into the wall | modest per centimeter — it wants thick walls | clay wraps every straw: a huge buffer, and clay's dryness protects the straw | heavy site labor, and it must *dry in the wall* — a slow, weather-watching job in a climate with no dry season |

And one filling is conspicuously absent: the logs themselves. Chapter 3 said it and it stays said — log mass is not insulation. A сруб wall on this terrain is structure and beauty and some warmth, but the numbers it offers are modest; what that means for the shortlist is faced in the worked example below.

### The skin: clay in, lime out

Plasters are not decoration here — they are working layers, and the two traditions divide the job exactly along the wool coat's grain.

**Clay plaster, inside.** Vapor-open, and better: clay is a moisture *flywheel* for the room — it drinks the humidity spike of a cooking evening and gives it back to dry winter air, quietly flattening the swings that condense on cold glass. It is endlessly repairable (a sponge and a trowel, no matching problem), it offgasses nothing, and under a hand it is warm and soft-matte in a way no paint achieves. Its honest limit: liquid water. Clay belongs where rain never lands.

**Lime plaster, outside.** Weather-shedding yet vapor-open — the rare combination the outer face demands — and alkaline enough that mold declines to live on it. Its micro-cracks heal themselves as the lime slowly re-carbonates. Its honest limits: it wants skilled hands and patient curing, and it forgives neither haste nor frost during the cure.

### The touch layer: oils, soaps, limewash

The innermost layer of the house is the one a child puts a cheek and a tongue against, and it gets chosen with that picture in mind. The house standard is short: penetrating oils and soap treatments for wood — they soak in and leave the surface *wood*, repairable patch by patch — and limewash over plasters. What fails the standard indoors: film-forming synthetic coatings where solid alternatives exist, and formaldehyde-bound sheet goods as interior surfaces — the five-year test's first question ("is there a natural option that does this job?") is answered *yes* almost everywhere inside a house, so conventional films rarely get past it there.

And the skepticism stays symmetric: "natural", "eco" and "bio" on a paint bucket are marketing exactly as much as any industrial claim. Read the ingredient declaration, not the leaf on the label — a "natural oil" cut with synthetic driers and solvents is judged by its contents, the same cold way we judged concrete in chapter 4. The bargain is with physics and with the child's lungs, never with a brand.

### Membranes, tapes and glues — the test, recalled

Recall **the five-year test** from chapter 4 — restate its two questions from memory before reading on. Now apply it where every natural-wall conversation eventually arrives: the plastic question.

The wind layer is where the test often lets natural win: a wood-fiber board, seams protected, with lime render or ventilated cladding over it, stops wind and sheds rain with no plastic in sight. But geometry is not always that kind — around openings, at junctions the boards cannot close cleanly, and later on the roof (chapter 8 will meet this at full scale), a membrane and its tapes may simply be the only thing that does the job and is still doing it in five years. Then it is admitted — openly, by name, without a green euphemism. The wool coat rule still governs the choice: whatever sheet enters the wall must be windproof *and* vapor-open, never a vapor barrier that turns the coat back into a raincoat. What stays out are the materials that fail the *indoor* test where honest alternatives exist — the formaldehyde-bound boards, the solvent films — because there the first question already answered itself.

One thing this beautiful, self-drying wall will never do: warm anyone. A vapor-open wall keeps itself dry — but nothing in it makes heat; what sits at the center of the plan? Chapter 9 puts a fire there.

## Worked example

Dress the worked terrain's shortlist — both circles, honestly.

**The frame, dressed.** From the kitchen outward: clay plaster on clay board; straw-clay or wood-fiber packed between the timbers; wood-fiber wind board closing the outside; then either lime render on the board or a ventilated larch cladding over an air gap. Now audit it with the rule, layer by layer: each layer more vapor-open than the one behind it — the wall dries outward, the diagram above is this exact wall. Walk one liter of kitchen steam through it: into the clay (buffered), on through the insulation as vapor, out through the wind board, gone on the wind behind the cladding. Nothing stops; nothing pools; the coat fits. The messy middle, honestly: the wind board's seams around the window openings do not close by good intentions — this is where the taped membrane strip enters the drawing, admitted by the test, three sentences ago.

**The сруб, dressed.** Different animal. The logs are structure and filling in one, so the coat concentrates where the wall is weakest: wool in every «паз (longitudinal groove)», corners cut tight — chapter 5 taught you to check both — and the settling years forbid any rigid skin for two years regardless of what we might want. Then the fork: leave the logs bare and accept what the numbers said (the stove will carry more, the winter bills say so), or — after settling — coat the outside in wood-fiber board and lime and let the logs be structure inside a wool coat. Both are defensible; they price differently and they *feel* differently, and this course will not pretend that second fact away. The worked terrain's final call happens in chapter 10, in the open, with the tradeoffs on the table.

**Straw-clay's schedule problem, named.** On a terrain with 1,450–1,500 mm of rain and no dry season, a filling that must dry *in the wall* is a bet against the sky. It is not disqualified — it is scheduled: packed in late spring, dried through the summer under a finished roof, checked before the first frost. Materials do not exist outside calendars here.

## Practice

**The return — the committal walk.** No new sheet today, by design: the checkpoint spends its practice budget taking the oldest sheet in the folder back to the bench. 40–60 minutes — on your land in real weather if you have it, at the table with the whole folder if you do not.

First, one retrieval to set the order of operations: recall **the scale of permanence** from chapter 2 (Yeomans' term) — before starting, restate its idea in one sentence: *what is hardest to change decides first.*

1. Lay out the whole folder: site sheet (ch. 2), shortlist (ch. 3), ground sheet (ch. 4), joinery card (ch. 5). This is the first time all of it works together.
2. Re-walk the site — or re-read the sheet — with four chapters of new eyes. Where does the cold-air river actually run? Where does water go, now that you know what drainage is? Does the footprint you penciled in chapter 2 still sit right, knowing what the ground under it must do?
3. Upgrade the site sheet: correct anything the later chapters have outgrown, and carry the ground sheet's drainage arrows and radon line onto it so the one sheet tells the whole ground story.
4. Decide, in permanence order — placement, then wall, then foundation: **footprint placement** (the hardest to ever change goes first); **wall system** (one of the two shortlist circles becomes *the* wall — the portraits, the grammar and today's coat are your grounds); **foundation type** (from the ground sheet, now that the wall's weight and temperament are known).
5. Write all three on the site sheet **in ink**, dated. Strike the pencil alternatives — a live "maybe" next to an inked decision is how folders rot.
6. If any gate-quiz question blanked this morning, follow its pointer *before* the ink. Commitments deserve a full deck.

**How to know it worked:** the site sheet carries three inked, dated commitments and no surviving pencil alternatives — the folder's oldest page now reads as a decision, not a survey.

**Short on time:** 10–15 minutes at the table — skip the walk, re-read the site sheet against the ground sheet, and ink the three decisions. A walk can be repeated any week; chapter 7's moisture plan cannot anchor to decisions that are not yet ink.
