## Your turn first

Before this chapter shows you anything, it asks you for everything. Here is the site one more time, in the dry numbers you first met in chapter 2: imagine a 17° slope at about 500 m, facing north and east; old village footprints on the slope's shoulder; clay over karst, wet ground and radon country; 8.7 °C mean, 117 frost days, records to −27.6 °C and a cold-air pool nearby that has touched −32.5 °C; 1,450–1,500 mm of rain with no dry season; snow on the ground about 63 days a year, up to a meter deep; a local roof tradition of 54°.

Now close the page and design the whole house. Where does it stand? What walls? What sits on the ground, what sits on the roof, what sits at the center of the plan? Take paper. Take twenty minutes. Argue with yourself.

This is the Summit, and it is supposed to feel like work — there are no new fundamentals left to hand you, only the harder job of making nine chapters of them agree with each other on one real-numbered slope. If your design and ours differ, that difference is the most useful text on this page. Read on when your sketch exists.

## The core

### Where the house goes — and what it costs

First, from memory, before reading on: why is the sheltered hollow — flat, calm, inviting — the worst offer this slope makes? Chapter 2 gave the reason a name. Say it, then check: the cold-air river. Cold air drains downhill like water and pools in the hollow; that pool is where the −32.5 °C record lives. A house there fights the site every night of every winter.

So the house goes where the old village footprints already sit: the shoulder. Above the parked river, below the hilltop's wind, on ground the old builders proved. But the shoulder is not free. It sits mid-slope, and the scale of permanence — Yeomans' order, recalled from chapter 2 — puts the access track *before* the house, because the track outlives every design change: it must climb to the shoulder, hold an excavator in the wet season, and stay passable through 63 days of snow. On this terrain, placement versus access is a real fight, and access wins concessions: the footprint slides along the shoulder toward the shortest workable track line, even though the view is better forty meters on. The site sheet decides with data, not with the view.

### Сруб or frame, decided in the open

The five walls of chapter 3 shortlisted two honest finalists for cold forest country: the сруб and the timber frame. Both are defensible here. Neither is a mistake. Watch the choice being reasoned, not revealed.

**The case for the сруб:** it *is* the local tradition; one trade raises structure and wall in a single act; the log wall is beautiful the day it's done and for a century after; and log mass genuinely evens out day-night swings.

**The case against it, on this site:** the frost pocket. Recall the wall portraits' hardest verdict — log mass is not insulation. A solid log wall on a −27 °C night is a modest coat, and thickening logs is a brutally expensive way to buy warmth. Then усадка (settling): the settling years mean the walls sink a hand's width over two years, and everything rigid — the masonry chimney, the 54° roof junctions, every window and door buck — must be designed and paid to move around them. And the craft: chapter 5's joinery card taught you to judge a чашка (corner notch/cup), and honest judging reveals how few hands still cut a true one within reach.

**The frame's case:** a post-and-beam frame stands still — no settling years, so the chimney, the steep roof and the joinery details get simpler and cheaper; and between its posts there is unlimited room for the wool coat — say its rule from memory before reading on: *windproof outside, vapor-open through, never a raincoat*. Deep wood-fiber insulation in a vapor-open assembly is exactly what a frost pocket asks for and exactly what a log wall cannot hold.

**The verdict, stated for this terrain:** the frame — for insulation depth, for standing still under a masonry chimney, for craft that is easier to find and to judge. And the reversal condition, stated just as plainly: a proven log builder within reach, a site out of the frost pocket, and a heart set on log walls make the сруб a defensible win. Two out of three of *climate, money, hands* said frame here. That is the whole argument.

### The messy middle — budget, усадка and the bargain collide

A synthesis chapter owes you the part where the design stops being elegant.

**Below grade, the bargain pays.** Recall the five-year test's exact wording before we spend it: conventional is allowed only where natural is absent or dies within about five years. Under this house it gets spent openly — frost-depth footings and the capillary break on wet clay are concrete-and-membrane territory, because everything natural placed there dies fast. The plinth above them is stone-faced and proud: knee height was chapter 4's floor, and a meter of leaning snow raises it — good boots first, and taller boots in snow country.

**The air plan costs a machine.** Chapter 7 left the HRV tension honestly open. On this site it closes: 117 frost days mean window ventilation throws heat away in quantities the stove cannot laugh off, and the damp ledger doesn't pause for winter — the household exhales its liters every day. Mold and stale air void the Gaia bargain; cracked windows at −27 °C are romance. So the worked house takes the heat-recovery ventilator, filter schedule written into the plan like a chore, not a footnote — a machine, admitted as one, serving the bargain better than the "natural" alternative would. The stove keeps its own room-sealed air, separate, as chapter 9 demanded.

**The roof keeps its tradition and its bill.** 54°, wide overhangs — the hat completing the boots — and wood shingles, because at this pitch a shingle roof outlives the five-year test many times over, so the bargain refuses the metal shortcut. Shingles cost real craft money, and here the budget finally collides with the square meters. The house answer is the course's oldest one, the cover promise itself: the smallest house that fits the life. When the money runs short, shrink the plan — not the wool coat, not the boots, not the shingles. A smaller warm, dry, breathing house beats a larger compromised one every year it stands. And if the budget still refuses, metal is the honest fallback — written down as a compromise the bargain tolerates, never green-washed into a preference.

**The heart stays put.** The stove stands at the plan's center, sized to the cold-air-river climate as chapter 9 taught, its foundation a fact of the ground works — the warm flywheel weighs tons before it warms anything.

### The order paper becomes timber

Chapter 8 planted this chapter's debt: *sheet by sheet the dossier is nearly a house — but in what order does paper become timber, and what gets built first?* Here is the payment, and the logic behind it is one sentence: **build in the order of the scale of permanence, and get dry before winter.**

The track and drainage come first — they outlast everything and every later stage drives over them. The ground works follow as one act: footings, plinth, perimeter drain, the radon layer and pipe under the future floor (chapter 4's kit told you whether this site needs it; on karst, assume yes until measured) — and the stove's footing poured *with* the house's, because tons of masonry cannot be given a foundation as an afterthought. Then the frame, then the roof — dry-in before autumn, because 1,450 mm of rain has no off season. The chimney rises with the shell, not with the stove: it penetrates the roof, so it gets flashed once, properly, and a standing chimney lets the finishing works be heated. Then the envelope, then the ducts and the stove's sealed intake before any lining closes, then the mason lays the mass — and the CO alarm is on the wall before the first fire, not after. Who signs what stays exactly where the Path put it: the engineer signs foundation, spans and snow load — Eurocode territory, judgment is yours, sign-off is not; the mason signs the stove and its flue against the local codes; the radon result and the CO alarm are yours to prove.

## Worked example

Here is the whole reconciliation compressed into the dossier's tenth sheet — the material schedule and build sequence for the karst house. Yours will differ in content; it should not differ in shape.

| # | Stage (in build order) | Materials on the schedule | Who signs / leads |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Access track + drainage cut above the footprint | gravel, culvert pipe | you + excavator |
| 2 | Ground works: frost-depth footings, stone-faced plinth, perimeter drain, capillary break, radon layer + pipe | concrete + membrane below grade (five-year test spent openly), local stone facing | **engineer signs** |
| 3 | Stove footing, poured with the foundation | concrete, rebar | engineer, mason consulted |
| 4 | Timber frame raised | spruce/fir posts and beams, larch sills on the plinth | carpenter leads; engineer signed the spans |
| 5 | Roof: 54° rafters, shingles, wide overhangs — dry-in before autumn | rafters, battens, split wood shingles | **engineer signs snow load**; roofer |
| 6 | Chimney rises with the shell, flashed once | masonry flue blocks or brick | **mason signs**, flue codes |
| 7 | The wool coat: wind barrier out, wood-fiber through, clay inside | wind barrier, wood-fiber insulation, clay plaster | you + carpenter |
| 8 | Air: HRV ducting + the stove's room-sealed intake, before linings close | ducts, intake pipe, HRV unit | installer; you hold the moisture plan |
| 9 | Stove mass laid; CO alarm on the wall before the first fire | firebrick, masonry, clay mortar; CO alarm | **mason signs the stove** |
| 10 | Finishes: limewash out, clay and oils in | lime, clay, fragrance-free oils and soaps | you |

Notice what the table quietly enforces. Every conventional material sits below grade or inside a machine, where the five-year test put it. Every signature lands on the stages that can kill or bankrupt — ground, spans, snow, fire. And the sequence has a season in it: stages 1–6 race the autumn; stages 7–10 can happen under a finished roof with a standing chimney warming the work.

One more harvest belongs to this chapter, and it is five chapters old. In chapter 1 you weighed a split billet and measured it across the grain; in chapter 5 you re-weighed it and wrote a prediction of where it would stop. Today it has stopped — or nearly. The final reading happens in the practice below, but its meaning belongs here: the billet quit losing weight when its moisture met the room's, and its width followed its water down, across the grain, exactly as chapter 1 claimed before you had evidence. You have now personally watched wood follow water for most of a season. The settling years are this same curve, scaled up to a wall — which is why your frame-versus-сруб verdict above was never abstract: you measured its physics on your own shelf.

## Practice

Away from the screen, dossier and billet on the table — 40–60 minutes.

1. **Harvest the wood log.** Weigh the billet, measure across the grain, write the final line next to chapter 5's prediction. Then write one sentence of verdict: how far off was your prediction, and in which direction?
2. **Spread every dossier sheet out at once** — site, shortlist, ground, joinery card, committal walk, moisture plan, roof, hearth. This is the first time the whole paper house has been in one field of view.
3. **Write the collision list in the margins:** placement vs access, walls vs budget, roof vs walls (does your wall choice move under your roof?), chimney vs settling, ventilation vs heat. For each collision, one line: what wins on your site, and why.
4. **Draw the tenth sheet:** your material schedule + build sequence, in the table shape above — build order, materials, who signs.
5. **Circle every row a professional signs.** If nothing is circled, re-read the ground, roof and hearth sheets — something is missing.

**How to know it worked:** a stranger picking up your tenth sheet could say what gets built first, from what, and who signs it — and your wood log ends with a prediction met by a measurement.

**Short on time:** harvest the wood log and number the build sequence on one sheet (10–15 min). The material columns can wait; the order cannot.
