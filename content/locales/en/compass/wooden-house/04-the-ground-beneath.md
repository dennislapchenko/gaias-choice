## A thaw in March

Imagine the worked terrain in the second week of March. The snow — it lay for two months, a meter deep at its worst — is going, all of it, in days. The clay underneath was already full before the melt began: this is 1,450-millimeter country with no dry season, and clay does not drink, it holds. So the water stands. Every dip in the old terraces is a gray mirror; the forest track is a canal; where you step off it, the ground takes your boot and considers keeping it.

And on the slope's shoulder, among the standing water, the old village footprints: rings of dry-laid stone, knee-high and higher, moss on their north faces. Step onto one. It is dry. The houses these rings carried are long gone — the timber above surrendered decades ago — but the stone is still keeping its one promise, to nobody, in the rain.

Chapter 2 planted a question and it is now due: *the old builders put stone under every house here, in a country made of trees — why stone, and why that much of it?* Here is the payment, and it starts with a retrieval. Recall **wood follows water** from chapter 1 — before reading on, try to say what your billet does when moisture comes and goes, and in which direction it moves most. Now look at the ground: it is the one direction from which water attacks *constantly*. Rain pauses; snow melts and is gone; but soil moisture is always there, climbing anything porous by capillarity, splashing up in every rain, freezing and shoving in every one of the 117 frost days. Wood in contact with ground is wood held at the wet end of its range, permanently — swollen, moving, and fed to every organism that eats damp timber. Stone does not care. It barely drinks, it does not rot, and it holds its dimensions whether March or August.

So the old builders spent their most expensive labor — moving stone without machines — on the least visible part of the house, and let the affordable material, wood, do everything above. That inversion is the whole lesson of this chapter: the ground work is bought once and judged for a century.

## The core

### Good boots, good hat — the boots half

The folk builders' rule, credited to tradition because it is one: **a house with good boots and a good hat forgives almost anything in between.** The hat — roof, pitch, overhangs — is chapter 8's whole subject. Today, the boots. The boots do three jobs, and all three are about giving water no route into wood:

1. **Height.** Ground moisture climbs porous material by capillarity, and rain splashes back up off the ground surface — the bottom third of a meter of anything standing outdoors gets wetted from below in every storm. So the wooden wall starts a knee's height off the ground at minimum, and more where snow sits against the house (here, snow reaches a meter — the plinth is also the wall's snow armor).
2. **The capillary break.** Stone wicks far less than brick or concrete, but "less" is not "none". Somewhere between mineral base and wooden wall there must be one layer water cannot climb at all. Tradition used slate or birch bark; today it is usually a bituminous or dense plastic strip — hold that thought, the five-year test below deals with it honestly.
3. **The drain.** The strongest move is not resisting water but removing it: give water somewhere better to go than your foundation, and most of the fight never happens.

One more retrieval before the diagram: recall **the mold triangle** from chapter 1 — name its three legs, and the only one you control. Ground contact hands moisture, the controllable leg, an unlimited supply. The boots exist to take that supply away.

### What wet clay over karst does

Our ground is a difficult marriage. **Clay** holds water against gravity, swells when wet, shrinks when dry — and in frost it does its worst trick: water in fine clay pores freezes into growing ice lenses that lift whatever sits above them, centimeters per winter, and never evenly. A footing that frost can reach on wet clay will be heaved, tilted, and cracked — slowly, then obviously. **Karst** underneath adds a lottery: bedrock at wildly uneven depth, dissolved voids, and sudden drainage through cracks, so that soaked clay pockets sit meters from bone-dry stone.

Frost depth is the design number, and here the site's biography matters. Recall **the cold-air river** from chapter 2 — before reading on, try to explain why our terrain runs colder than the regional map admits. A frost pocket that has recorded −27.6 °C, with pools nearby to −32.5 °C, drives frost deeper than the average map line for the region. The rule: the footing bears below the depth frost can reach, or on ground drained well enough not to heave — and the exact local number is not this course's to give. Foundations, bearing and frost depth are code territory; a competent local engineer answers with the map, the margin and a signature, and asking that question early is itself the competence test for everyone involved — you included.

### The foundation table

Four honest options for wet clay over karst, with their losses stated. This table goes onto the ground sheet — it is the dossier's instrument for this chapter.

| Option | How it works | On wet clay over karst | Where it honestly loses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dry-laid stone plinth** (the old way) | stone stacked without mortar; drains through itself; endlessly repairable | wants firm, drained bearing — sinks slowly into soft wet clay unless it stands on a proper footing | enormous skilled labor; no code paper by itself; find the mason first |
| **Stone or masonry piers** (point footings) | the house stands on stools; ground touched only at points; air moves under the floor | each pier must reach firm ground — karst makes that depth a per-pier lottery | cold floor without serious insulation; bracing on a 17° slope is engineer's work |
| **Concrete strip footing + stone-faced plinth** | modern footing below frost, traditional knee-high boot above grade | the workhorse: predictable on clay, and the visible house still reads as stone | real money and real embodied energy; buried forever — drainage must come first |
| **Insulated slab / raft** | the house floats on a stiff plate over foam | shrugs at uneven bearing; gives a warm floor | the most concrete and plastic of all, under the house for its whole life — hardest to square with the bargain |

### The five-year test, formally born

Chapter 1 named the clause inside the Gaia bargain; today it becomes a working tool you will use in every remaining chapter. Two questions, asked of one specific spot in the design:

1. **Is there a natural option that does this job, here?**
2. **Would it still be doing the job five years from now, in this spot?**

Both answers yes — natural wins, no debate. Either answer no — a conventional material is admitted, openly, without green paint over the decision. The test cuts both ways: it stops eco-romantic choices that rot in three winters, and it stops lazy defaults to concrete where stone would stand a century.

Below grade is where the test earns its keep, because below grade natural honestly loses more often than anywhere else in the house. Stone is a fine natural answer *above* ground — the old footprints prove it — but the footing under it on wet clay, and the capillary break on top of it, have no natural option that passes question 2: no moss, no bark, no tar-free trick both bears a house and shrugs off permanently wet, freezing dirt for decades. So the footing is concrete and the break is a bituminous strip, said plainly. And the skepticism is symmetric: "eco-concrete" and "natural waterproofing" marketing get exactly the scrutiny industry marketing gets — the bargain is with physics, not with labels.

### Drainage — somewhere better to go

Half the pocket rule, and the cheaper half. On a 17° slope the ground sends its water *at* the house from above, all year, in a climate with no dry season. The moves, in order of leverage:

- **Grade the surface** so the first meter around the plinth falls away from it, on every side.
- **Intercept the slope** with a curtain drain — a gravel-and-pipe trench across the uphill side that catches subsurface water and walks it around the house to daylight downslope. On clay this one trench does more than any waterproofing product ever will.
- **Throw the roof's water clear** — gutters or a generous drip apron; the hat's contribution, detailed in chapter 8.

Design the drainage *before* the foundation, not after the first flooded spring. Water removal beats water resistance; a dry footing on modest stone outlives a wet footing in heroic concrete.

And notice what we have and have not done. We have kept the ground's water out — yet most mold grows from water made *inside* the house; where does it come from, and how does it leave? That question is chapter 7's, and tonight's new habit below starts collecting your own evidence for it.

### Radon on karst

One more thing rises from this ground, and it is not water. Radon is a radioactive gas from the slow decay of uranium in rock and soil; karst, fissured and cavernous, moves it upward unusually well, and this terrain sits squarely in radon country. Established building guidance treats it seriously, and so does this course — without drama and without diagnoses: it is a design input, like frost depth.

The rule is **test first, and design the path regardless.** On land with no building yet, assume the regional radon class is high (here, it is) and put the mitigation path on the ground sheet while it costs a pencil line: a sealed membrane across the ground under the floor — the five-year test admits it instantly, since no natural sheet stops a gas — plus either a ventilated sub-floor void or a capped rough-in pipe under the slab that a fan can join later if the finished house tests high. Then test the finished house with a passive detector over months, not a weekend gadget reading. Retrofitting a radon system through a finished floor costs a hundred times the pencil line.

## Worked example

Founding on the shoulder — imagine it, sheet in hand.

The bench where the old footprints sit is the obvious candidate from chapter 2's site sheet, and the first honest complication appears immediately: a relict terrace is partly *made* ground — old fill, uneven, resting on clay. The old builders answer from their graves: their rings sit where the bench meets native slope, not on the soft outer lip. Noted on the sheet.

Now walk the table. Dry-laid stone alone: beautiful, and it loses — soft wet clay under a full perimeter, and where is the mason who has laid a hundred meters of it? Piers: tempting, less material, the bargain smiling — but twelve separate excavations into karst is twelve lotteries, the floor hangs cold in a frost-pocket winter, and a braced pier grid on 17° is exactly the drawing an engineer redlines. The raft: it would work, and it pours more concrete than every other option combined — question 1 of the test says stone can do the visible job, so the raft loses the argument here. The choice falls where it usually falls on ground like this: **concrete strip footing below the engineer's frost line, stone-faced plinth to knee height and a hand above it** — snow reaches a meter here, so the north face gets more boot than the code asks. Capillary break strip on the plinth top, under the first timber.

Then the water: curtain drain across the uphill side, daylighting at the terrace edge where the slope already wants to carry water away; surface graded off the plinth all round. Then the radon line: regional class high, membrane under the floor, rough-in pipe capped in the sub-floor, "test finished house, winter, 3 months" written where it cannot be forgotten.

The messy middle, stated: nothing above is adventurous, and that is the point. The ground is where this course is most conservative, because the ground is bought once, buried, and judged by the decade — every gram of romance spent down here is paid for upstairs, with interest, in rot you cannot see until it is structural.

## Practice

The ground sheet — 30–45 minutes at the table, plus ten minutes at a window tomorrow morning. If your land is real and reachable, walk it in the worst weather you can get (chapter 2's rule still stands); otherwise work the worked terrain.

1. Copy or trace your site sheet's building area onto a fresh sheet — this becomes **the ground sheet**.
2. Mark where water is now: low spots, standing water, the uphill direction, anywhere moss or rushes tell on the drainage. In rain if at all possible.
3. Walk the foundation table against your ground and **circle one row**. Write the losing rows' one-line reasons for losing — future-you will re-litigate this without notes.
4. Draw the drainage: arrows for surface grading, a line for the curtain drain if the slope asks for one, and the point where the water daylights — *somewhere better to go* must be an actual place on the sheet.
5. Write the radon line: your region's radon class (your national radon map has it), "membrane + sub-floor path" if it is elevated, and "test finished house, 3 months, heating season".
6. File the sheet in the folder, behind the shortlist.
7. Start **the sill watch**: tape a card and pencil by the coldest window in your current home. Each morning, one line — room · outside weather · what the glass and sill show (dry / misted edge / beads / pooled) · what ran the evening before (cooking, laundry, bath, guests, drying clothes). That is all. Chapter 7 will read this card with you.

**How to know it worked:** the ground sheet carries one circled foundation with the losers' reasons written, arrows that lead water to a named better place, and a radon line — and the sill card has tomorrow's first entry on it.

**Short on time:** 10–15 minutes — circle the foundation row, write the radon line, and tape the sill card to the window tonight. The drainage arrows can wait for the weekend; the morning log cannot, because its value is the unbroken streak.
