## Write down why

You did not arrive at this course wall-less. Almost nobody does. Somewhere
in your head is a picture — honey-colored log walls in lamplight, or a
timber-frame hall with its bones showing, or the neat economy of a framed
and clad box. Fine. Every build starts as somebody's picture.

Now the question this chapter opens with, and it is a fair one: **write
down, in three lines, why that wall.** Not why that house, not why that
life — why that wall system, over the other ways of stacking wood into a
weather-tight plane.

Try it before reading on. If your three lines are about your climate, your
money, or your hands — good; this chapter will sharpen them. If they are
about a photograph — you are normal, and this chapter exists for you. The
question underneath is the one a builder will eventually ask across a
table: *why this one and not the other four?* Right now you probably cannot
answer it. By the end of the chapter you will — either with your picture,
now armed with reasons, or with a different wall you can defend better.

## The promise

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- read any wall system through **the wall portrait** — six fixed lines that
  make different walls comparable;
- describe how each of the five approaches carries load, handles water, and
  spends skill and money — and where each honestly loses;
- correct the two loudest myths of wooden building on the spot: log mass is
  not insulation, and a stud frame is not cheap rubbish;
- fill the comparison table for your own climate, money, and hands, and
  circle **the approach shortlist** — the two walls that survive.

## The core

### The wall portrait

Five approaches are coming, and they will not be reviewed — they will be
portrayed, each in the same six fixed lines: **what it is · how it carries
load · how it handles water · skill and time it demands · money · where it
honestly loses.** Call the format **the wall portrait**. The fixed order is
the entire point: comparison is only honest when every candidate answers
the same questions in the same order, and the last line is mandatory — a
wall with no named loss has not been examined, only admired.

Two retrieval pulls from chapter 1 before the gallery opens, because two of
the six lines stand on them. First: **wood follows water** — recall which
way wood moves more, along the grain or across it, before reading on.
Across, by far — and every "how it handles water" line below is that fact
wearing a different costume. Second: **the Gaia bargain** — every "where it
honestly loses" line is written under its veto: a wall that closes the mold
triangle is out of this course, however beautiful its photograph.

### Сруб (stacked-log house)

**What it is.** Whole logs laid horizontally, corners interlocked with
notches, each log grooved along its underside to sit tight on the one
below. The oldest wooden wall of the northern forest belt — the 150-year
house from chapter 1 is one.

```diagram wall-srub
aria: "Elevation of a stacked-log wall corner: six log courses, the cross-wall log ends showing as ringed circles at the interlocked corner, log ends standing proud past the crossing, a grooved joint line under every course"
t1: "Сруб — every log is structure"
p1: "Corner cup; ends stand proud"
p2: "Groove (паз) under each log"
p3: "Each course carries all above"
note: "Openings interrupt the structure — old срубs keep windows few and small"
caption: "The circles at the corner are the exposed end grain the water line names — this wall's weak point stands at its showiest joint."
```

**How it carries load.** The wall *is* the structure: every log is a beam,
every course carries everything above it. Openings are interruptions the
wall must be persuaded to accept, which is why old срубs have few and small
windows.

**How it handles water.** Better than its reputation. Solid, vapor-open
wood with enormous mass buffers wetting and releases it — the wall survives
getting damp *if it can dry*. Its weak points are specific: the bottom
courses near ground splash, and the exposed end grain at the corners. And
wood follows water at wall scale: logs shrink across the grain as they dry,
so the whole wall settles visibly through its first years — every door,
window, and chimney must be designed expecting it.

**Skill and time it demands.** Log craft is real, scarce, and priced
accordingly. And the wall spends time twice: once to cut and raise, then
seasons of settling before finishing can honestly begin.

**Money.** In forest country the material can stand nearly on site; the
hands are the cost. Per heated square meter, rarely the cheap option
anymore — the craft that was once every village's is now a specialist's.

**Where it honestly loses.** Warmth. **Log mass is not insulation:** a
30 cm log wall insulates roughly like 10 cm of wood-fiber batt — real, but
far below what a −27 °C winter asks of a modern envelope. Mass smooths the
swings between day and night; it does not stop steady cold. A bare сруб in
this course's climate needs its stove to forgive its wall, every day of
winter.

### Timber frame (post-and-beam)

**What it is.** A skeleton of few, large posts and beams joined by
carpentry. The wall between the posts is not structure — it is filling and
wrapping, chosen separately, which makes this the one approach that splits
"holds the roof up" from "keeps the weather out" into two independent jobs.

```diagram wall-timber-frame
aria: "A timber frame in elevation: two heavy posts under a beam on a sill, corner braces, and a light dashed infill panel between the posts that carries no load"
t1: "Timber frame — skeleton apart from skin"
p1: "Posts carry all the load"
p2: "Beam, joined by carpentry"
p3: "Brace; infill holds nothing"
note: "The dashed wall is chosen separately — as thick and vapor-open as needed"
caption: "Two independent jobs in one picture: the heavy members hold the roof up, and everything dashed only keeps the weather out."
```

**How it carries load.** Concentrated: loads run down the posts to a
handful of points. Openings are nearly free — the wall between posts holds
nothing — and the roof can stand before any wall exists, a real gift in a
1,450 mm climate.

**How it handles water.** The frame itself never meets weather; the
envelope decides everything. That is power and burden at once: the wall can
be exactly as thick and as vapor-open as your climate asks — or exactly as
wrong as your detailing, with the frame's own honesty unable to save it.

**Skill and time it demands.** Real joinery skill — scarcer than stud
framing, but alive, teachable, and hireable. Once raised, weather-tight
fast.

**Money.** Large timbers and skilled joints cost up front; the freely
chosen insulation depth pays it back every winter after. Middle of the
field, honestly accounted.

**Where it honestly loses.** It is two designs, not one: the frame *and*
the envelope, plus every interface between them. More decisions, more ways
to be invisibly wrong — a badly built wrap hides its condensation inside
the wall for years before the smell reports it.

### Light stud frame

**What it is.** Many small studs at close spacing, sheathed into a rigid
skin — the default wall of the modern world, from suburbs to site sheds.

```diagram wall-stud-frame
aria: "A light stud-frame wall in elevation: many thin studs at close spacing between top and bottom plates, insulation batts filling the open bays, and a sheathing panel bracing one face"
t1: "Light stud frame — many small members"
p1: "Studs: no single one matters"
p2: "Sheathing braces the whole"
p3: "Insulation between the studs"
note: "Redundancy is real — a weak stud has neighbors"
caption: "Thin sections everywhere the eye lands: nothing here has mass to buffer a leak, so every layer trusts the detailing."
```

**How it carries load.** Distributed: no single member matters much, the
sheathing braces the whole, and the redundancy is genuine — a weak stud has
neighbors.

**How it handles water.** Least forgivingly of the five. Thin sections have
no mass to buffer a leak: they dry fast and rot fast, so the detailing
carries everything, all the time.

**Skill and time it demands.** The lowest threshold here: any competent
crew anywhere can build it, and it is the most owner-builder-friendly wall
per hour of skill you must acquire.

**Money.** The cheapest per square meter and per unit of warmth. No
contest, and pretending otherwise is romance.

**Where it honestly loses.** Margin and defaults. Small sections forgive
few mistakes; and the industry's standard recipe — glued sheathing panels,
plastic films, foam — fails the Gaia bargain before the first nail. Say the
verdict plainly: a stud frame is **not** cheap rubbish; honestly built, it
is the workhorse of natural building today. But nothing about it is honest
*automatically* — a bargain-keeping stud wall must be re-specified natural,
piece by piece.

### Cordwood

**What it is.** Short logs laid across the wall like stacked firewood,
bedded in lime or clay mortar, end grain showing on both faces.

```diagram wall-cordwood
aria: "A cordwood wall face-on: short log ends showing as ringed circles packed into a mortar matrix, framed by posts and beams that carry the loads"
t1: "Cordwood — firewood laid into mortar"
p1: "End grain faces the weather"
p2: "Lime or clay mortar bed"
p3: "Frame carries the real loads"
note: "Wood and mortar move to different rules — joints open as moisture swings"
caption: "What the drawing makes plain: nearly the whole face is circles — this wall greets the rain almost entirely with end grain."
```

**How it carries load.** The mortar matrix and log ends together — and
modestly. Best practice puts cordwood as infill inside a frame that carries
the real loads, and this course agrees with best practice.

**How it handles water.** Here is its hard problem: end grain drinks water
an order of magnitude faster than side grain — and this wall shows end
grain to the weather on both faces. Worse, wood and mortar move to
different rules as moisture swings (wood follows water; mortar does not),
so hairline joints open around every log and invite the rain in.

**Skill and time it demands.** Low skill, high patience — genuinely
beginner-friendly masonry, one log at a time. The wood must be very dry
before it goes in, which means the wall starts a year before the wall
starts.

**Money.** The cheapest material list of the five — waste wood and mortar —
paid for in hours, and in maintenance forever after.

**Where it honestly loses.** Climate — specifically this course's. Under
1,450–1,500 mm of rain with no dry season, exposed end grain plus moving
joints is a maintenance subscription, not a wall. Cordwood earns a niche —
the sauna, the workshop, the garden wall under a generous roof — not a
crown.

### Roundwood framing

**What it is.** Timber framing with unmilled poles: the tree's round
section used whole, fibers uncut, bark off.

```diagram wall-roundwood
aria: "A roundwood frame: two unmilled tapering poles carrying a round beam, the beam's end grain visible as a ringed circle, scribed joints marked where beam saddles each pole"
t1: "Roundwood framing — the tree used whole"
p1: "Round section, fibers uncut"
p2: "Scribed to its exact partner"
p3: "Taper — no two poles alike"
note: "Thinnings and coppice — wood a sawmill rejects — can hold up a roof"
caption: "No two members in this drawing share a dimension — which is exactly what a span table cannot describe."
```

**How it carries load.** Like a timber frame — a skeleton. A round pole is
stronger for its diameter than the beam milled out of it, because milling
severs the fibers that grew continuous; that is why thinnings and coppice —
wood a sawmill rejects — can honestly hold up a roof.

**How it handles water.** As any frame: the envelope decides, the skeleton
stays inside and dry.

**Skill and time it demands.** The slowest craft in this gallery. No two
poles are alike, so every joint is scribed to fit its exact partners —
beautiful, meditative work that refuses to be hurried.

**Money.** Material nearly free where forest is overstood — the worked
terrain's leggy hazel and dense thinnings are candidates — while the cost
concentrates in time and scribing skill.

**Where it honestly loses.** Paper. No standard sections means no standard
span tables, and the engineer who must sign your structure — chapter 1's
scope line stands — has little to sign against. Spans stay modest,
approvals move slowly. A niche — outbuildings, roofs, the barn you will
love — not a crown.

### The table that decides

Here is the gallery compressed into the shortlist instrument — one row per
wall, the portrait's middle lines as columns:

| Wall | Carries load | Handles water | Skill · time | Money | Where it honestly loses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Сруб (stacked log)** | the wall itself — every log is structure | mass buffers wetting; survives if it can dry; corners and bottom courses weak | scarce, dear craft; builds once, then settles for seasons | material near-free in forest country, hands dear | warmth — log mass is not insulation |
| **Timber frame** | a skeleton of posts and beams; the wall carries nothing | frame never sees weather — the wrap decides everything | real joinery, hireable; weather-tight fast | timbers and joints up front, insulation depth pays back | two designs to get right, and their interfaces |
| **Light stud frame** | many small studs + bracing skin; redundancy | least forgiving: thin wood dries fast, rots fast, detailing carries all | lowest threshold; most owner-builder-friendly | cheapest per m² and per unit of warmth | margin, and defaults that fail the bargain |
| **Cordwood** | mortar matrix + log ends; best as infill in a frame | end grain to the weather both faces; joints open as wood moves | low skill, high patience; very dry wood first | cheapest materials, paid in hours and upkeep | a no-dry-season climate |
| **Roundwood framing** | pole skeleton; round fibers uncut, strong per diameter | as any frame — the envelope decides | scribed joints, every one bespoke; slow | thinnings near-free, skill and time dear | no span tables — engineers hesitate, spans stay modest |

The table becomes an instrument the moment you add your three rows beneath
it: **your climate** (the site sheet's numbers), **your money** (one honest
figure), **your hands** (hours, skills, patience — owned or hired). Then
give each wall three verdicts — yes or no from climate, from money, from
hands — and the pocket rule at the end of this chapter tells you how many
yeses a wall needs. All three.

## Worked example

Imagine running the instrument on the worked terrain, the table copied into
the folder under the site sheet.

Climate row first — and a retrieval pull from chapter 2: why must the
design temperature come from the hollow and not the weather station? Recall
it, then check: the site sits in a frost pocket — **the cold-air river**
parks on it, so the wall designs against the pool's −32.5 °C, not the
station's −27.6 °C. Add 1,450–1,500 mm of rain with no dry season and some
63 days of snow. Climate's verdicts: cordwood — no, end grain in a climate
that never dries. Roundwood — no as the house wall (spans and sign-off),
noted warmly for outbuildings. Stud frame — yes, with insulation depth
chosen freely. Timber frame — yes, same freedom. Сруб — a qualified yes:
the wall handles the wet honestly, but against this cold its warmth debt
must be paid by envelope or stove, and the design must say which before the
yes counts.

Money row: imagine standing timber on site — сруб and roundwood material
nearly free, but the scarce craft dear; stud frame cheapest end to end;
timber frame in the middle, its joinery bill softened by on-site timber.
Hands row: imagine more patience than crew — a household ready to hire the
skilled core and do the filling and finishing itself. That favors the
frames and the сруб-with-hired-cutters, and it does not fear slow.

Score it and the terrain circles two: **сруб and timber frame** — the first
because material, tradition, and water-handling all say yes and only the
warmth debt argues, the second because it says yes everywhere and asks only
for design discipline. Light stud frame is written directly beneath them as
the honest fallback if money collapses — three quiet yeses beat romance.
Both circled walls are defensible; this course will not pick between them
today. The Summit makes that call on this terrain, in the open, with the
whole dossier on the table.

And one line under the shortlist, filed where you will trip on it: both
circled walls end the same way — as bones waiting for a body. The frame you
shortlisted is an empty skeleton — what fills and wraps it without turning
the wall into a raincoat? Chapter 6 answers.

## Practice

At the table with the folder, away from the screen — 30–45 minutes.

1. Copy the comparison table into the folder **by hand**. Slower than
   printing, and that is the point: hand-copying forces you through every
   cell once.
2. Under it, write your three rows: **climate** — the hardest numbers from
   your site sheet (design cold, rain, snow, dry season or none); **money** —
   one honest figure you could spend without borrowing your future;
   **hands** — hours per week, skills you own, skills you would hire.
3. Give each of the five walls three verdicts — yes or no from climate,
   from money, from hands. No maybes: a maybe is a no that wants to be
   liked.
4. Circle the top two. This is **the approach shortlist**.
5. Under each circled wall, write one sentence: *what would have to be true
   for this one to lose.* If you cannot name its losing condition, you have
   admired it, not chosen it.

**How to know it worked:** the shortlist names two walls, each with three
recorded yeses and one written sentence that could kill it — reasons on
paper, not a photograph in your head.

**Short on time:** skip the full copy — score only the climate and hands
verdicts for all five walls and circle two provisionally. Ten minutes, and
the shortlist exists for the money row to confirm later.

- Name, for each circled wall, the condition that would make it lose.
