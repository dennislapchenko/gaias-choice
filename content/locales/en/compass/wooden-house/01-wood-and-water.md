## Two houses, one village

Imagine a village street in wooded hill country. On the left stands a
сруб (stacked-log house) — walls of whole logs gone nearly black with a
century and a half of weather. Step inside and the air is dry. Knock on the
wall and it rings. One hundred and fifty years old: no membranes, no foam,
no engineered anything, and the wood is sound to the core.

On the right, its neighbor: twenty years old, built by the modern book —
insulated, wrapped, tight, warm at half the firewood. Pull the wardrobe away
from the north wall and there they are: black corners. You smell it before
you see it — damp paper, still air.

Be honest about the sample before drawing any lesson from it. The old houses
that got water wrong rotted, burned, and fell, and nobody walks you past
their absence; the street shows you the survivors, not the odds. Old ways
were not better by default — most old houses are gone.

But the survivors survived for reasons, and the reasons are knowable,
physical, and learnable. They kept wood away from the ground. They gave rain
somewhere better to go. And whatever did get wet could dry faster than rot
could use it. The new house, for all its clever materials, closed a trap the
old one never closed. This course is those reasons made deliberate — so that
the house you design is dry by design, not dry by luck.

## The core

### Wood follows water

Wood is hygroscopic: it exchanges moisture with the air around it, always
drifting toward equilibrium with wherever it lives. A board in a heated room
dries and shrinks; the same board in a damp shed swells. Dimension tracks
moisture, permanently — wood never stops doing this, not after a year, not
after a century.

And it does not move evenly. Along the grain — down the length of the trunk —
wood barely moves at all. Across the grain, it moves a lot: several percent
of its width between soaked and dry. That one asymmetry explains most of
what you will ever see wood do: boards cup, logs split along their length as
the outside dries faster than the core, joints that were tight in October
rattle in February, and a wall of stacked logs visibly loses height as it
dries — each log shrinking across its grain, the whole stack following.

Durability tracks the same variable. Rot is not age; it is fungi digesting
wood, and fungi drink before they eat. Keep solid wood below roughly twenty
percent moisture content and decay stalls — the spores are present and
simply cannot work. That is the 150-year wall's whole secret: not magic old
timber, but a century and a half spent mostly dry.

Name the fact and keep it: **wood follows water** — dimension and durability
both track moisture. Every question this course asks about a wooden building
will turn out to be a water question wearing a costume.

### The mold triangle

Mold needs three things at once: **moisture, warmth, and food**. Call it
**the mold triangle**, and look at your options corner by corner.

Food you cannot remove. Mold eats wood, paper, dust, skin flakes, the paper
facing on plasterboard — a house is a buffet no cleaning schedule empties.

Warmth you do not want to remove. Heat is the entire point of the house; a
cold house is not a strategy, it is a failure with a different name.

Moisture is the only corner you control. That is the whole discipline of
this course compressed into one sentence: every wall, foundation, roof, and
stove decision ahead is a move in moisture management, whatever else it
also is.

Now rerun the black corners from the street. Warm room air carries water
vapor. The corner behind the wardrobe is the wall's coldest spot, with no
air moving across it. Vapor condenses on cold surfaces. Paper-faced board is
food. Triangle closed — not by cheap materials or lazy builders, but by
geometry and physics doing exactly what they always do. The old house never
closed it: massive vapor-open walls with no cold pockets behind furniture,
wood held off the ground, and enough air moving through to carry the
household's moisture away.

One line on health, and only one, because this course makes no medical
claims: we treat mold strictly through established building science and the
toxin lens — a moldy house is a failed house, full stop, and nothing in
later chapters is allowed to argue with that.

### The Gaia bargain

Here is the rule the whole course keeps, stated once and enforced everywhere:
**natural materials as far as they honestly work — never one step past the
point where the building physics fails.** Wood, stone, clay, lime, wool,
straw — preferred, always, and not only for the chemistry: they are
materials a family can live inside without wondering what the walls are
offgassing.

The bargain has a veto clause, and the veto has no exceptions: **mold and
stale air void the deal.** A "natural" house that grows mold or holds its
own exhaled air has failed at the only job that justifies the word natural —
keeping the people inside it well. When naturalness and physics collide,
physics wins, and the design changes until they stop colliding.

The bargain cuts the other way too. Where no natural material honestly does
a job — or where the natural option dies in a few years and drags the
assembly down with it — the bargain admits the conventional material,
openly, without green-washing around it. Call that clause **the five-year
test**; chapter 4 turns it from a clause into a working instrument you can
hold any product against.

And one standing discipline inherited from the rest of this site: symmetric
skepticism. A label that says "natural," "breathable," or "eco" earns
exactly as much scrutiny as one that says "high-tech." Claims get tested —
by numbers, by physics, and later in this course by your own measurements —
not believed.

## Worked example

Every worked example in this course happens on one recurring site, so meet
it now. Imagine a homestead parcel in the Dinaric karst — Kočevsko-type
country, Slovenia. Around 500 m elevation; a 17° slope facing mostly north
and east; relict terraces and the stone footprints of an old village on the
slope's shoulder; dense forest with a 15–25 m canopy and overstood hazel;
brown bears in the woods. Clay soils over karst — ground that holds water,
in a bedrock country known for radon. Climate: 8.7 °C annual mean, 117
frost days, records to −27.6 °C, and cold-air pools nearby measured to
−32.5 °C — the site itself sits in a frost pocket. Rain: 1,450–1,500 mm a
year with no dry season. Snow cover about 63 days, up to a meter deep.

Those numbers are real regional data; the site is honestly hypothetical.
Now read it through this chapter's three concepts, because the terrain does
that reading for you all course long.

Wood follows water: 1,450 mm of rain with no dry season means the water
question never closes — there is no season when the site dries out and
forgives a mistake. Every wooden surface will spend the year hunting its
equilibrium in damp air, so every detail must assume movement and wetting.

The mold triangle: 117 frost days and a frost pocket mean a long season of
warm-inside, brutally-cold-outside — the exact gradient that condenses
indoor vapor on every cold surface. The moisture corner of the triangle will
be under pressure from October to April, from both sides of the wall.

The Gaia bargain: wet clay underfoot and radon in the bedrock are two places
where the bargain will be tested hardest — chapter 4 shows where natural
materials serve and where the five-year test honestly admits conventional
ones below grade.

One thing this terrain cannot do is size your house — and that decision
starts the artifact. Nobody builds a house in eleven chapters; everybody can
build the document a real build starts from. **The paper house** is a
physical folder in which you design the whole house on paper before the
first log is cut. Every chapter adds one named sheet; by chapter 11 it is a
dossier you could put on a carpenter's table and be taken seriously. It
opens with a cover promise: **"the smallest house that fits the life."**
Not the largest house the budget allows — the smallest one the actual life
needs, with rooms named by what happens in them, not by catalog convention.
Fewer square meters is fewer logs, fewer joints, less to heat, less to go
wrong: buy less, buy better, at architectural scale.

## Practice

Away from the screen — 30–40 minutes tonight. Two jobs: open the folder,
open the wood log.

1. Find a physical folder — cardboard, ring-bound, anything that survives
   two months of handling. On the cover write the promise: **"the smallest
   house that fits the life."**
2. Under it, write three lines: the activities your house must hold daily,
   weekly, and rarely. Activities, not rooms — "bread, laundry, a child
   sleeping through a storm," not "kitchen, utility, nursery."
3. Take a split billet of firewood — or any bare piece of solid wood the
   size of your forearm. Weigh it on a kitchen scale, to the gram.
4. Measure its width **across the grain** with a ruler or caliper, to the
   millimeter. Mark the measuring spot with a pencil line so every future
   measurement lands on the same place.
5. Start the wood-log sheet: today's date, the weight, the width, and where
   the billet lives (room, distance from the heat source).
6. Write a prediction, dated: in two months, will it be heavier or lighter,
   wider or narrower — and by how much? Commit to numbers. Being wrong on
   paper is the point.
7. Put the billet somewhere you pass daily and the sheet into the folder.

**How to know it worked:** the folder exists with the cover promise on it,
and the wood log holds one dated weight, one width, and one prediction
concrete enough to be wrong.

**Short on time:** skip the folder for today — one sheet of paper titled
with the cover promise, then weigh, measure, and predict the billet. Ten
minutes, and the paper house has its first page.
