## The dossier on the table

Imagine the evening this course was built toward. The folder lies on the table, thick now: a site sheet with ink commitments, a shortlist with two circles, a ground sheet, a joinery card, a moisture plan, a roof sheet, a hearth sheet, a build sequence. Outside it is raining, because on this terrain it is usually raining. Nothing is built. Not one log is cut.

This is not a sad scene. It is the strongest position a future builder can hold: every expensive mistake this house could make has already been made and corrected where mistakes cost pencil. But paper has met its limit. Paper cannot cut a joint, lay a flue, or tell you whether the man quoting you a price can do either. The distance between this evening and the first cut is not more chapters — it is people, sources, and years of your own measurements. Ten chapters taught the vital fifth of this craft; this last one makes you work at a different job: learning how you will learn the other four-fifths without us.

## The promise

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- interview a carpenter or a stove mason and judge work you cannot yet do, using the joinery card as the instrument;
- build a small shelf of sources actually worth their weight, and say what disqualifies the rest;
- test any claim — a mason's, a book's, a natural-product label's — against your own logs instead of anyone's word;
- close the paper house: judge the finished dossier against chapter 1's cover promise, and copy the crib in as its last page.

## The core

### The mason interview — the joinery card earns its keep

Chapter 9 planted this chapter's debt: *a stove like this asks for a mason — how do you find one, and how do you judge work you can't yet do?* The answer is the oldest trick of the informed amateur: you cannot out-craft a professional, but you can make them explain, and explanation cannot be faked as easily as a finished photo.

Pull out the joinery card. Before reading on, say its three сруб (stacked-log house) words from memory — the course named them in chapter 5. Now check yourself: венец (crown/course), паз (longitudinal groove), чашка (corner notch/cup). That card was never really for you. It is an interview sheet:

- **Ask them to name the joint.** Point at their own work — a corner, a post head — and ask what the joint is called and why it is there and not another. A master answers with reasons; a pretender answers with adjectives. The pocket rule cuts both ways: if *they* can't name the joint, they can't be trusted with the work.
- **Ask what усадка (settling) will do to it.** For a log builder: how much, over how long, and what moves around it — window bucks, door frames, the chimney gap. Recall from memory what the settling years demand of a chimney before you ask; then listen for whether their answer matches what you know. A builder who waves settling away has not finished a сруб twice, which means they have not finished one.
- **Ask to see work ten winters old.** Anyone's work is handsome the summer it's done. Water, frost and settling do the honest reviewing, so references from old winters beat portfolios from last month. Look at the lowest venets, the corners, the sills — the places chapter 4 taught you water goes first.
- **Listen for water.** A good master talks about water without being asked — where it lands, where it drains, what it will ruin. If an hour passes and water has not come up, the interview is over.

For the stove mason, add the two questions that have no negotiable answers. Where does the fire's air come from? — the only good answer is its own sealed duct, because the fire gets its own air or it takes yours. And how does the flue meet the codes and the CO alarm? A mason who calls the alarm optional, or the room's air "plenty", has disqualified themselves — not on craft, on safety, and safety is the one place this course allows no second interview.

### A shelf worth its weight

The remaining depth of this craft lives in sources, and most sources are marketing wearing a roof. Three filters keep the shelf short and honest:

- **Numbers and failure cases beat finished photos.** A source worth shelf space shows measurements, details drawn at the scale where water lives, and — the surest sign — autopsies of its own failures. A source that has never been wrong has never been checked.
- **Codes and standards are free and unglamorous — start there.** Your national building code and the timber and stove standards it points to are the one genre with no product to sell you. They read like plumbing manuals; so does water damage.
- **Prefer sources older than the product they'd have to sell.** Craft literature written before a material had a marketing department describes the material's behavior, not its brochure. Where old craft and modern building science disagree, that disagreement is exactly the interesting part — read both and take the dispute, not a side.

All three filters compress to one question: *does this source ever say "we were wrong"?* Keep the ones that do.

### Your logs are the referee

Everything above still leaves you taking someone's word. The course's real graduation gift is that you no longer have to: you own two instruments, and they are cheap to keep running.

The wood log proved you can put a claim on a scale and wait. The sill watch proved your own house produces data every morning. So when any claim reaches you — a mason's "this needs no gap", a book's "this wall never needs a barrier", a label's "natural insulation, breathes, never molds" — the reflex the course hopes you keep is: *what would this look like in my log?* Buy the sample board before the pallet. Weigh it, wet it, watch it through a season, the way you watched the billet. Write the prediction first, the verdict after.

And apply the skepticism symmetrically — this deserves saying plainly in a course that loves natural materials. "Natural" is a marketing department too. A wood-fiber brochure, a sheep's-wool distributor, a clay-paint label all get exactly the scrutiny a cement brochure gets: the Gaia bargain — recall its veto clause before reading on — was never "natural wins"; it was *natural as far as possible, never past the point of physics failure*, with mold and stale air voiding any deal, whatever the label says. Your ledger arbitrates, not the shade of green on the bag.

### Our own next step, stated as a plan

One disclosure, because this course has kept its accounts honest for ten chapters and will not stop on the last page. We have land in Dinaric karst country — the same kind of ground this course kept imagining. The dossier method you have just finished is not a teaching device we built for you and will shelve; it is the method we intend to run ourselves, folder, logs, interviews and all. That is a plan, not a biography — nothing is built, and this course will not pretend otherwise. If timber ever follows the paper, it will be written down as it happens, not before.

## Worked example

Two closings, and then the folder is done.

**First: judge the paper house against its own cover promise.** On the folder's first page, chapter 1 made you write it: *the smallest house that fits the life*. Read it aloud. Now leaf through every sheet asking one question of each: does this serve the life, or the square meters? The guest room used four nights a year, the second bathroom, the meter of roof that exists for proportion — every sheet that answers "the square meters" gets a pencil question mark, and every question mark is money returned to the wool coat, the shingles, the stove. A dossier passes the promise not by being small, but by containing nothing it cannot defend. Write the verdict under the promise line, one sentence, dated.

## Practice

Table, folder, pen — 30–45 minutes, no screen once you've read the steps.

1. **Copy the crib by hand** onto the folder's last page — every named concept's line, every pocket rule verbatim. Hand-copying is not ceremony; it is the last retrieval exercise, and you will notice which lines you no longer need to look at.
2. **Judge the dossier against the cover promise.** Read the promise aloud, leaf every sheet, pencil a question mark on whatever serves the square meters instead of the life. Write the one-sentence verdict under the promise, dated.
3. **Write the "can't-yet-judge" list** — three things in your dossier you would want a professional's eyes on before money moves (a span, a flue detail, a drainage fall). This list is your first interview agenda.
4. **Draft the mason's interview sheet** from the joinery card: the name-the-joint question, the усадка question, the ten-winters question, the water listen — and, for the stove, the two no-negotiation questions.

**How to know it worked:** the folder now ends with the crib in your own handwriting, and its first page carries a dated verdict — the paper house is closed, judged, and ready to be argued over with a real builder.

**Short on time:** copy the eight pocket rules onto the last page and write the verdict line (10–15 min). The concept lines can join over the week; the verdict should not wait.

## Now you can

- Interview a carpenter or stove mason and read the answers — joints named, settling respected, old winters shown, water mentioned unprompted, safety non-negotiable.
- Build and defend a short shelf: numbers over photos, codes before brochures, sources that admit error.
- Put any claim — traditional, scientific, or natural-marketing — in front of your own logs and let the measurement answer.
- Close a design against its founding promise and say, in one dated sentence, whether it kept its word.

## Next

There is no chapter 12, and the folder does not need one. What comes next is not reading.

Keep the two instruments running — a wood log for every material that wants your money, the sill watch wherever you live now; a year of your own data outweighs any brochure that year produces. Walk old houses the way chapter 2 taught you to walk land: in bad weather, looking at boots, hats and corners. Put the can't-yet-judge list in front of an engineer before anything is signed, and the interview sheet in front of a mason before anything is promised. And when land, money and hands finally say yes together — the folder on your table is the first honest conversation of the build, already written.

The paper house is done. The wooden one is patient.
