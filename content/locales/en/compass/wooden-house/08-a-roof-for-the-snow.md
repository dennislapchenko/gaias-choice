## A meter of snow in one week

Imagine the worked terrain in early February. The snow that has fallen in a single week now stands a meter deep on the clearing — the regional records say this is not an exceptional winter, just a real one, and the cover will lie for two months of its roughly sixty-three days. A meter of settled, wet snow is not a postcard; it is a load measured in hundreds of kilograms on every square meter it rests on. Then March: a thaw afternoon, and somewhere upslope a whole roof's worth of it lets go at once and comes down like a slow, heavy wave — off the roof, past the eave, onto whatever stood below.

Now look at the old village roofs on this terrain — the footprints your site sheet recorded back in chapter 2 belonged to houses whose roofs stood at **54 degrees**. Not 30, not 40. Fifty-four. Nobody surveyed their way to that number; generations of collapsed and surviving roofs did. This chapter is about reading what that number knows, and then making your own roof decision the way this course makes decisions: with an instrument, not a mood. The roof is the last big piece your paper house is missing, and it is the one piece where a wrong guess arrives all at once, in February, from above.

## The core

### The three jobs of a hat

Chapter 4 gave you the folk rule — **good boots, good hat** — and filled only the boots, leaving three callouts pointed at an empty roof with "ch. 8" written on them. Before reading on, recall from memory what the boots consisted of: the plinth, the break, the drain. Today the hat completes, and it has exactly three jobs:

1. **Throw water past the boots.** Wide overhangs are the plinth's upstairs partner: every centimeter of overhang is rain that never touches the wall and splash that never reaches the sill logs. On a wall meant to dry — recall the wool coat rule from chapter 6 before reading on — the overhang is what keeps the coat from being soaked faster than it can dry.
2. **Manage the snow, one way or the other.** A roof either *holds* its snow all winter (shallow pitch, strong structure, rough covering) or *sheds* it (steep pitch, slick covering). Both are legitimate strategies; the disaster is the roof that does neither on purpose — holding until a thaw, then shedding a season's load in one slide onto the doorstep. This is why pitch is a snow decision first: 54° is the terrain's tradition because at that angle snow leaves early and often, in small harmless portions, and the load case the structure must survive stays small.
3. **Close the coat from above — coldly.** The roof over an insulated ceiling should stay a *cold* roof: a ventilated air gap under the covering keeps the underside near outdoor temperature, so the snow on it does not melt from below, refreeze at the eave, and dam the meltwater into the assembly. The wool coat rule extends overhead: windproof and rain-tight outside, vapor-open and ventilated beneath.

### The decision table

A covering is where money, tradition and physics collide, so we decide it the way a protocol decides: one table, seven columns, every candidate walked through all of them. No column may be skipped — a covering that wins six and fails one can fail the house.

| Covering | Min. pitch | Weight | Fire | Lifespan | Snow behavior | Cost | The five-year test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood shingle | steep (the steeper, the longer it lives) | light | honest weakness — distance to flues, sparks respected | decades at steep pitch, years at shallow | holds light snow, sheds at steep pitch; quiet | high if bought, moderate if a local maker exists | the natural option itself — passes where pitch lets it dry |
| Clay tile | moderate and up | heavy — the structure must be sized for it | excellent | very long | holds snow well; needs snow-rated fixing on steep roofs | high, plus the heavier structure under it | mineral and honest — passes |
| Standing-seam metal | almost any | very light | excellent | long | sheds suddenly and completely — snow guards mandatory over doors and paths; loud in rain | moderate | conventional — allowed only where natural options honestly fail |
| Bitumen shingle | moderate | light | poor — and it ages toward brittle | shortest of the four | holds; melts free unevenly | lowest upfront | fails where any option above is affordable — a repeat purchase pretending to be a roof |

Two columns deserve a word. *Snow behavior* is the column beginners skip and mountain builders read first: a slick roof does not remove the snow problem, it relocates it — to the ground, all at once, wherever the eave points. And the last column is chapter 4's **five-year test** doing exactly the job it was born for — try to recall its clause before reading on: conventional materials are allowed only where the natural option is absent or dies within about five years. At a 54° pitch, wood shingle dries fast and lives long: natural passes, so natural plays. On a 20° roof the same shingle stays damp and dies young: there the test itself opens the door to metal, honestly and without green guilt.

### Three houses through the table

A protocol proves itself on cases, so here are three — all imagined, all contrasting.

**Case one: the worked terrain.** Imagine the committed house from your chapter 6 walk: the frost pocket, sixty-three days of snow cover, forest all around, a meter of maximum depth. Tradition already voted 54°. The table seconds it: at that pitch, shingle's lifespan column turns green, the snow column says "sheds early, in portions," and the fire column demands only discipline around the flue — noted for chapter 9. Clay tile also passes, at real cost in structure and money. Metal passes every physics column but must justify itself against the five-year test — and here it cannot, because shingle honestly works. **Verdict: wood shingle at 54°, clay tile the defensible second, metal the honest third if the shingle-maker cannot be found.** Three defensible roofs, one reasoned order — that is what a protocol produces.

**Case two: the same family, half the money.** Imagine the budget collapses: the shingle-maker quotes a number that ends the conversation, and tile's heavier roof structure adds timber the money no longer buys. Walk the table again with the cost column weighted honestly. Metal now wins — light, affordable, long-lived — and the five-year test *permits* it, because the natural options have become absent in the only sense that matters: unreachable. The table then hands back its conditions: snow guards over every door and path, because standing-seam sheds like an avalanche; and acoustic honesty — the family should hear a rainstorm on metal once before signing. **Verdict: metal, eyes open, guards drawn.** The Gaia bargain — recall its terms from chapter 1 — is not offended: it never demanded natural at any price; it demanded honesty about the trade.

**Case three: a gentle-climate reader.** Imagine a reader of this course on a mild lowland site: rare snow, warm wet winters, a wide 25° roof already framed. Wood shingle at 25° in a wet climate sits damp and fails the five-year test *as the natural option* — it would die young. Clay tile passes at that pitch and climate and takes the crown; bitumen loses to it on every column but the first price. **Verdict: tile — and the lesson that the same table, honestly walked, gives different houses different roofs.** The instrument travels; the answer does not.

### A roof on walls that move

Now chapter 5's debt. It left you with: *the walls will sink a hand's width over two years — what happens to a roof built on walls that move?* Recall **the settling years** before reading on — усадка (settling), the reason a сруб (stacked-log house) is finished twice. If your committed wall system is a сруб, the roof arrives during the settling years, and a roof nailed rigidly to walls that are sinking is a slow-motion structural argument: rafters pushed out of plane, ridge lines bowing, joints prying themselves open.

The craft answer is to let the roof *ride*: rafter feet on sliding fixings rather than rigid nailing, so the rafter can change its angle a degree or two as the walls come down; screw-jack compensators under any posts that carry roof loads, adjusted season by season; and generous, flexible clearances wherever anything that does not settle — a masonry chimney, most of all — passes through everything that does. Chapter 9 will lean on that last clause hard. A timber-frame or stud-frame house largely escapes this chapter of the drama — its settling is millimeters, not centimeters — which is one more line for the honest ledger your chapter 3 shortlist started.

### The engineer signs

And now the hard line, stated once and without softening. Everything above teaches judgment: pitch strategy, covering choice, snow behavior, overhang reach. **What it does not teach — and will not — is structural sizing.** The snow load your rafters, purlins and ridge must carry on this terrain is Eurocode territory: mapped snow zones, altitude corrections, drift and slide cases, safety factors — a body of engineering with a professional's name at the bottom. A course can make you a good client; it cannot make you the signature. Your roof sheet therefore ends with a note *addressed to* a structural engineer: site, altitude, chosen pitch, chosen covering and its weight, the span you hope for — and the request that they size and sign the structure. Design the roof; hand over the load. That division of labor is not a disclaimer, it is the design.

One seed for later, because you can feel it coming: sheet by sheet the dossier is nearly a house — **but in what order does paper become timber, and what gets built first?** That reckoning is chapter 10.

## Worked example

The roof sheet for the worked terrain, drawn start to finish — imagine it as this evening's work at the table. Title: *"Roof — case one decided."* First line: **pitch 54°**, with the reason written out, because a dossier records reasons, not just answers: *"sheds snow early and in portions; matches the surviving local roofs; lets shingle dry."* Second: **covering — wood shingle**, with the runner-up order from the table copied beneath it (*tile, then metal + guards*) — a dossier that keeps its second choices survives its first budget shock. Third: **overhangs** — drawn wide enough that the drip line lands clear of the plinth, with the chapter 4 boots sketched under them so the whole hat-and-boots rule sits on one page.

Then the messy middle, recorded rather than smoothed: the fire column's condition (*shingle keeps its distance from the flue — final geometry waits for chapter 9's stove placement*), the settling note (*rafter feet slide; nothing rigid ties roof to wall until the settling years end*), and at the bottom, boxed, the part that is not ours: **"Snow load: Eurocode — engineer sizes and signs. This sheet is the brief, not the calculation."** The sheet is one page, and every line on it either decides something or names who decides.

## Practice

At the table, away from the screen — 30–45 minutes. You are producing the **roof sheet**, the dossier's eighth piece.

1. Copy the decision table's seven column headers onto a fresh sheet, sideways if you need the room.
2. Walk **your** house through it — the one you committed in chapter 6, on your site or the worked terrain. Fill every cell honestly; write "don't know yet" where that is the truth.
3. Decide and record: **pitch** (with its snow strategy — hold or shed — named in words), **covering** (with the runner-up order kept), **overhang reach** (drawn, with the drip line clear of your plinth).
4. Add the settling clause if your wall system settles: one line on how the roof rides.
5. Box the engineer's note at the bottom: site, altitude, pitch, covering weight, intended span — and the sentence "structure to be sized and signed by a structural engineer."
6. Clip the sheet into the folder, facing the moisture plan.

**How to know it worked:** your roof sheet contains at least one decision made *against* your first taste — a covering or pitch the table talked you out of — with the reason written next to it.

**Short on time:** 10–15 minutes — draw only the boxed engineer's note and the pitch line with its snow strategy named. Those two lines are the roof sheet's skeleton; the table walk can join them next sitting.
