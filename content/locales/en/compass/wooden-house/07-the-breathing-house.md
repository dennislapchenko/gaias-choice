## The walls never breathed

Here is the belief, stated as fairly as we can, because most of us carried it here: *old houses were healthy because their walls breathed.* A century-old сруб (stacked-log house) smells of wood, not of mildew; it never had a ventilation system; therefore the walls themselves must have exchanged the air, and the modern sealed house is sick precisely because we wrapped it in plastic and stopped them. Half of that story is true, which is what keeps the whole of it alive.

Now the autopsy. A wall of solid wood passes almost no air — that is the point of caulking every joint with moss or wool, and the old builders did it obsessively. What a vapor-open wall passes is water vapor by diffusion, and diffusion is slow: through a whole wall, it moves *grams* of water a day. A household, as we are about to count, makes *liters*. The walls could not have ventilated the house any more than a wick could drain a bathtub.

What old houses actually had was leaks — gappy windows, loose doors, and above all a fire burning most of the day, pulling air up the chimney like a whole-house extract fan made of flame. The leaks changed the air, generously. They also cost brutally: the same drafts that dried the wall froze the family, and the fire ate wood all winter paying for it. So the wall stayed dry and the house stayed fresh — not because the wall breathed, but because the house leaked and the fire covered the bill.

"Vapor-open," the property chapter 6 taught you to build, buys exactly one thing: **a wall that can dry itself**. It has never dried a *house*. Those are two different jobs, and this chapter is about the second one — because the water that mold grows on is mostly made indoors, by us, every day.

## The core

### The water made indoors

Chapter 4 left a debt on the table, and today it comes due: *we've kept the ground's water out — yet most mold grows from water made inside the house; where does it come from, and how does it leave?*

It comes from the family. Breathing alone puts out roughly a liter per adult per day. Cooking adds more — every uncovered pot is a humidifier. A shower, a floor wash, laundry drying on a rack, a crowd of guests, a houseplant collection: each is a line in the same account. Building science has counted this for decades, and the total for an ordinary household lands **on the order of ten liters of water a day**, every day, all winter. Call this account **the damp ledger**. You will read your own in a few pages — you have been collecting its data every morning since chapter 4 without knowing the name.

The ledger explains a fact that surprises most people: ventilation standards are sized by moisture, not by oxygen or CO₂. Long before stale air harms anyone, ten daily liters of vapor will find the coldest surface in the house and become liquid on it. Recall the first law this course gave you — **wood follows water**, from chapter 1; try to say from memory what it promised about wood and dampness before reading on. The same law runs indoors: the vapor you exhale tonight is dimension change, finish failure and mold food tomorrow, unless it leaves the building first.

### The mold triangle, put to work

You met **the mold triangle** in chapter 1 — before reading on, name its three corners from memory, and name the only one you control. Moisture, warmth, food: spores need all three. Food is everywhere — dust, wood, plaster, the paper face of any board. Warmth is everywhere you intend to live. So the whole fight is one corner: **moisture**, and now we can formalize what "controlling moisture" means in practice. Two levers, no third:

1. **Keep surfaces warm.** Condensation happens where warm moist air touches a surface below its dew point. Cold spots — a thermal bridge, a gap in the wool coat, the corner behind a wardrobe on an outside wall — are where the ledger's liters land first. The fix is continuity of insulation and furniture that lets air reach the wall.
2. **Move the vapor out before it finds a cold spot.** That is ventilation, and it is the rest of this chapter.

This is also where mold *actually* starts, and it is predictably boring: the coldest surface of the wettest room. Window reveals. The bathroom ceiling corner farthest from the door. Behind furniture on north walls. An unheated bedroom where someone sleeps with the door closed — one sleeper, one closed door, one cold night is a wet window by morning. Your sill watch has been photographing exactly this mechanism: the window pane is the coldest surface in most rooms, so it weeps first. It is the house's early-warning gauge, and you own weeks of its readings.

### Airtight and vapor-open are allies

The misconception we opened with has a stubborn second life: if vapor-open is good, surely airtight is bad? No — and this is the one distinction worth rehearsing until it is reflex. Try to recall **the wool coat rule** from chapter 6 word for word before reading on — windproof outside, vapor-open through, never a raincoat. Airtightness is the same idea seen from indoors: air must not *leak through the assembly*, because leaking air is a freight train for moisture. A small gap that lets warm room air seep into the wall carries orders of magnitude more water into the assembly than diffusion ever will — and drops it, as condensation, exactly where you cannot see it. Diffusion is the wall drying politely through open materials; leakage is the ledger being poured into the timber.

So the two properties are allies with divided labor: **airtight keeps the ledger's water out of the wall; vapor-open lets whatever still arrives dry away.** Neither ventilates the house — the house gets ventilated on purpose, through openings you chose, not cracks you didn't. ### Moving the air on purpose

Now the honest accounting, because ventilation is not free. Every liter of vapor leaves the house riding on warm air, and the heat in that air leaves with it. In a house wearing a proper wool coat, this flips the usual intuition: **the air changes can lose more heat than the walls do.** The better your envelope, the larger the share of your firewood that goes out the vent. There is no cheating this — a house that keeps all its warm air keeps all its water, and the mold triangle collects. The only choices are *how* the air moves and whether any heat is recovered on the way out:

| Approach | What it does well | What it honestly costs | The Gaia bargain says |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purge airing (windows wide, minutes at a time) | Free, simple, instant; fine backup for cooking spikes | Depends on discipline twice a day, every day; brutal on the coldest weeks — exactly when the ledger peaks | Natural and honest, but a habit is not a system |
| Trickle vents + stack effect | No machine, no power; steady background change | Weak in mild weather, strong in cold (driven by the very temperature difference that makes it costly); little control | Passes where climate is gentle and the ledger light |
| Extract fans (kitchen, bath) + inlet vents | Removes vapor at the source, when it is made; cheap, proven | Slight suction on the whole house — on karst that suction can pull soil gas from below; heat still fully lost | Acceptable; the radon caveat must be answered first |
| Balanced HRV (heat-recovery ventilation) | Constant fresh air with most of the heat kept; no suction; filters pollen and dust | A machine: ducts, power, a filter schedule, embodied plastic; fails dirty if neglected | The open tension — see below |

Two lines from earlier chapters cut across this table. First, radon: recall your ground sheet from chapter 4 — the terrain is karst, the test kit and the sub-floor mitigation path are already on that sheet. An extract-only system holds the house at slight under-pressure, and under-pressure is an invitation to soil gas. If the radon test says the ground is generous with it, that alone can veto extract-only and push toward balanced ventilation. The floor plan and the air plan are one plan.

Second, **the Gaia bargain** — say its veto clause from memory before reading on: mold and stale air void the deal. Here it meets its hardest case. An HRV is unmistakably a machine — ducts, a fan, filters bought in plastic sleeves. Nothing about it is natural. And yet in a tight house in a serious climate, it may keep the air fresher and the heating honest better than any natural habit sustained through a −27 °C January. The bargain does not automatically reject it; the bargain rejects *mold and stale air*, and the HRV may be the thing that prevents both. **We leave this tension open on purpose.** Your damp ledger — the real one, in your data — is the judge: a light ledger in a mild climate never needs the machine; a heavy ledger in a frost pocket may find the machine is the natural choice's best servant.

And file this chapter's one forward question, because the table just made it unavoidable: ventilation throws warmth away with the wet air — **what heat source is generous enough to afford honest airflow?** That is chapter 9, and the empty middle of your floor plan is already waiting for it.

## Worked example

Imagine the worked terrain in deep January: 117 frost days in the year, the frost pocket doing its work, no dry season to lend a drying month. The committed plan from chapter 6's walk lies on the table — footprint on the shoulder, wall system chosen, plinth drawn. A family of three lives in the imagined house, and their ledger runs the usual ten-ish liters a day; outside it is −20 °C, so every window is a dew-point trap and "just crack a window" costs a stove-load of heat a night.

We draw the moisture plan for it — one sheet, three ingredients. Top left: the wall section, small — the drawing chapter 6 taught you finally gets its permanent home. Center: the floor plan with **air paths** — fresh air entering at the bedrooms and living room, crossing under the doors, extracted where the water is made: kitchen and bathroom. The air walks the house from dry rooms to wet rooms, never the reverse. Then the **risk rooms**, marked honestly: the north corner bedroom (coldest surfaces, sleeping people, closed door), the bathroom (the ledger's single largest deposits).

And here is the messy middle, drawn rather than hidden: the first draft of the air paths collides with itself. The extract wants suction; the radon note on the ground sheet warns against suction; the woodstove we have not yet chosen will want air of its own and tolerate suction worst of all. We write the collision on the sheet instead of resolving it prematurely — *"extract vs radon vs stove air — resolve in ch. 9"* — because a dossier that records its open questions is worth more than one that hides them. The HRV column stays open too, with a pencil note: *"decide by the ledger."*

## Practice

At the kitchen table, away from the screen — 40–60 minutes, and bring the sill-watch log you have kept since chapter 4. Tonight it becomes the damp ledger, read in your own handwriting.

1. Lay the sill-watch log out and **circle every morning the windows wept**.
2. For each circled morning, write one line naming the evening before: laundry on the rack, a long shower, guests, a big pot cooking, a closed bedroom door. Patterns will surface fast — this is the damp ledger, no longer an idea but your data.
3. Take one clean sheet, title it **"Moisture plan."** Copy your wall section small into the top left corner.
4. Sketch your floor plan (current home or the paper house — the skill transfers). Mark the **risk rooms**: for each wet room, ask "where is its coldest surface?" and put an X there.
5. Draw the **air paths** as arrows: where fresh air should enter, how it crosses, where it should leave. Extract belongs where water is made.
6. At the bottom, write the open verdict: what your ledger says so far about how much ventilation your household actually needs, and whether the HRV question leans yes or no on your evidence. Date it.

**How to know it worked:** a stranger picking up the sheet could point to where your household makes its water, which surface catches it first, and which way the air is supposed to carry it out.

**Short on time:** 10–15 minutes — do steps 1 and 2 only: circle the wept mornings and write the one-line cause beside each. File it in the folder titled "damp ledger, first reading" — the moisture plan can grow around it next sitting.
