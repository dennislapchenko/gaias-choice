## The empty square in the middle

Open your dossier and lay the floor plan on top. It has survived seven chapters of decisions: the footprint committed in ink, the wall system chosen, the air paths arrowed, the roof decided and briefed to an engineer. And in the middle of it — you have been drawing around it since chapter 6 — sits an empty square with a question mark in it: **"heat?"**

That square is now the weakest thing in the folder, and not quietly. The moisture plan's arrows assume warm air worth moving. The roof sheet's fire column left its distance-to-flue condition dangling on a flue that does not exist. Two chapters have written promissory notes against this square, and both come due today. Chapter 6 asked it first: *a vapor-open wall keeps itself dry — but nothing in it makes heat; what sits at the center of the plan?* Chapter 7 sharpened it: *ventilation throws warmth away with the wet air — what heat source is generous enough to afford honest airflow?*

One answer pays both debts, and it is the oldest serious one wooden houses in cold country have: not a radiator, not a unit, but a piece of masonry architecture — a stove you build the house around, not into.

## The core

### The stove that answers two questions

The **подовая печь** is defined by an absence: it has **no grate**. The fire burns directly on a thick masonry hearth floor, and the wood is lit from the *top* of the stack, not the bottom. That sounds like a detail; it is the whole machine. A grate feeds a fire from below — air rushes up through the coals, the burn races, and half-burnt gases bolt for the chimney still full of fuel. On a bare hearth with top-down lighting, the flame eats downward into the stack at its own pace: the wood below is preheated, the gases rise *through* the flame and finish burning, and the combustion runs closer to complete than an open system gets. Cleaner glass, cleaner flue, ash to carry out a couple of times a year rather than every morning.

Then the second half of the machine: the hot gases are not allowed to leave. They wind through channels inside the masonry mass, giving up heat to brick meter by meter, and reach the flue merely warm. The heat stays in the stove; the stove is the house's radiator for the next day.

### The warm flywheel

Name the new concept: **the warm flywheel**. Tons of masonry — and a real stove of this kind weighs tons — store the heat of one fierce, clean burn and release it slowly through their surface for many hours. Two burns a day, morning and evening, and the stove's flanks stay gently warm around the clock: no roaring-hot stove at midnight, no cold house at dawn, but a flat, patient warmth the way a flywheel flattens an engine's pulses.

This is the precise answer to chapter 7's debt. Recall your own **damp ledger** — try to remember its daily total before reading on — and the uncomfortable arithmetic that honest ventilation throws warmth away with the wet air. A heat source affords that honesty when its heat is cheap, steady and already banked. The flywheel is all three: the burn is efficient, the warmth is stored rather than chased, and airing the house for ten minutes does not "waste the fire" — the fire is in the brick, not the air. A house heated by a flywheel can afford to breathe.

The flywheel's cost is its virtue inverted: **slowness**. A cold masonry stove takes hours to wake; it cannot rescue a house the way a blast of metal-stove heat can. It answers a rhythm — the two-burn day — not a whim. And it is heavy and expensive twice over: the mass itself, and the foundation that must carry the mass. The stove's footing is part of its price, planned back in the ground it stands on.

### The center of the plan

Where does it stand? In the middle — and now the reasons stack. A stove radiates in all directions, so a central stove heats rooms *through* the walls it shares instead of throwing half its warmth at an exterior wall. The chimney rises shortest and straightest from the center through the ridge — the roof sheet's flue-distance condition is easiest to honor there. And the masonry core becomes the plan's anchor: kitchen on one flank, living room on another, a warm wall for the bedroom — the floor plan organizes itself around the warm mass the way the course said the house would.

Sizing is a climate decision, and you already own the instrument. Recall **the cold-air river** from chapter 2 — say from memory why the worked terrain's hollow reaches −32.5 °C before reading on. A stove is sized for the frost pocket's worst week, not the season's average: the flywheel must carry the house through the nights when the parked river is at its coldest and the damp ledger still demands its air changes. On a terrain with 117 frost days and records near −28 °C, that sizing conversation is exactly what the mason — a word we will return to — is for.

### The fire's air, the family's air

Now the safety lines, and they are lines, not suggestions.

**The fire gets its own air.** A burn consumes a large, continuous stream of air, and it will take it from wherever is easiest. In a leaky old house that was the drafts; in your airtight, wool-coated house the fire and the family would be breathing from the same sealed vessel — with the ventilation system, the extract fan and the chimney all pulling on it at once. The moisture plan already recorded this collision. The resolution is a **room-sealed air supply**: a dedicated duct from outside straight into the firebox, so the fire's appetite never touches the room's air balance. The imagined stove in the diagram has it drawn under the floor — mint, sealed, non-negotiable.

**The CO alarm is non-negotiable.** Carbon monoxide is the failure mode of every flame in every house, no matter how well built; it is odorless, and the only honest defense is a working alarm placed per its instructions and tested. This course treats the alarm like chapter 6 treated the wind barrier: not an upgrade — a component. No alarm, no fire.

**The chimney never rides the walls.** Recall **the settling years** from chapter 5 — усадка (settling) will bring a сруб (stacked-log house) down a hand's width while the masonry chimney, standing on its own foundation, does not move a millimeter. Everything where the two systems meet must slide: clearances around the chimney at every floor and roof penetration, flashing that rides the roof as it comes down, and never — never — a timber member resting on the chimney or a chimney load resting on timber. The flue, like the snow load, also has codes and a professional behind it: flue sizing, clearances to combustibles and the sweep's access are the mason's and the chimney codes' territory. The course teaches you to ask; it does not license you to improvise.

And here this chapter plants its one seed, because you can already feel the dependency: a stove like this is not bought, it is *built*, and built well by few. **A stove like this asks for a mason — how do you find one, and how do you judge work you can't yet do?** That is chapter 11's territory, and your joinery card already hints at the method.

### The honest alternatives

The Gaia bargain — recall its veto clause from chapter 1 before reading this table — does not crown the masonry stove by default. It asks each candidate the same two questions: does it keep the house warm and the air honest, and what does it truly cost?

| Heat source | What it honestly gives | What it honestly costs | The Gaia bargain says |
|---|---|---|---|
| Подовая печь (hearth-bottom masonry) | Clean, near-complete burns; round-the-clock stored warmth; affords honest ventilation; a warm heart the plan organizes around | Tons, real money, its own foundation, a scarce mason, hours to wake from cold | The natural fit for a lived-in cold-country house — if the budget and the mason exist |
| Metal stove | Cheap, everywhere, installs in a day; heat in minutes — unbeatable from cold | Hungry — fast burns, more wood; hot-cold swings; scorching surfaces near children; heat dies with the flame | The honest budget answer, respected, not mocked — many good houses start here |
| Grated masonry stove | Most of the flywheel with a more common firebox; masons easier to find | Faster, less complete burns than the hearth floor; ash more often; still heavy and founded | A fine second — far closer to the flywheel than to the metal box |
| Heat pump | Remarkable efficiency; no flue, no fuel store, no ash; thermostat-flat warmth | Electricity-dependent on a remote terrain; outdoor unit noise; refrigerants and electronics — a machine through and through | Passes only where wood honestly fails — no woodland, no mason, no one home to tend a fire |

Read the last column as a set of *conditions*, not a ranking. A family with woodland, presence and patience meets the masonry stove's conditions; a family heating a weekend house meets the heat pump's or the metal stove's. The bargain vetoes mold and stale air — it has never vetoed honesty about money, and the metal stove is often the honest first winter of a house whose flywheel comes later.

## Worked example

The hearth sheet for the worked terrain, drawn this evening — imagine it. Floor plan in the center of the page, and the empty square finally filled: the stove drawn at the junction of kitchen and living room, its warm flank forming one bedroom wall. Around it, the sheet collects every claim this chapter made, each as one drawn line. The **foundation outline** under the stove, dashed down into the ground sheet's territory, with a note: *"own footing — mass in tons; sized with the mason."* The **air duct** from the north wall under the floor to the firebox: *"room-sealed — the fire never breathes the room."* The **chimney** rising center-ridge, ringed with clearance at every penetration: *"chimney does not settle; everything around it slides — see roof sheet."* The **CO alarm** marked as a component, not an afterthought. And the sizing line: *"size for the frost pocket's worst week, not the mean — cold-air river, ch. 2."*

The messy middle goes on the sheet too, because it is real: the central stove wants the shortest chimney, but the committed floor plan from chapter 6 put the bathroom where the stove's third flank would love to be — warm bathroom versus warm bedroom is a genuine argument, and the sheet records it as an open pair with the family's answer pencilled, not inked. And one boxed honesty at the bottom: *"first winter may be the metal stove — flywheel when the mason and the money align."* A dossier that can say that out loud is a dossier the Gaia bargain trusts.

## Practice

At the table, away from the screen — 30–45 minutes. You are producing the **hearth sheet**, the dossier's ninth piece.

1. Copy your floor plan small onto a fresh sheet and find its center of gravity — the point that touches the most lived-in rooms. Draw the stove square there, honestly sized (a masonry stove is furniture-large, not radiator-small).
2. Draw the **air duct**: from outside, sealed, into the firebox. Write "room-sealed" on it.
3. Draw the **chimney** to the ridge. Ring every floor and roof penetration and write "slides — nothing rests on the chimney, the chimney rests on nothing" beside the rings if your walls settle.
4. Mark the **foundation** under the stove with its own outline and the note "own footing, sized with the mason."
5. Place the **CO alarm** on the plan as a drawn component.
6. Fill the sizing line: your climate's worst week (your site sheet has the numbers), and which alternative from the table is your honest plan A — with plan B named if plan A waits on money or a mason.

**How to know it worked:** the empty square is gone — and every line touching the stove names either a safety rule or the professional who owns it.

**Short on time:** 10–15 minutes — draw only the stove square at the plan's center and the sealed air duct, and write the CO-alarm line. The heart and its lungs first; the rest of the sheet can grow next sitting.
