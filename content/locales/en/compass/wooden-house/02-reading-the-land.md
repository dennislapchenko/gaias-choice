## The sheltered hollow

Walk almost any sloped, wooded parcel with building on your mind, and one
place will call to you. Everyone finds it. The flat clearing down in the
hollow, ringed by trees, out of the wind. Level ground — a cheap, easy
foundation. Shelter — warmth, surely. Privacy, birdsong, that held-in-cupped-
hands feeling. Estate listings price it as a feature; generations of weekend
cabins stand in exactly such places.

State the belief fairly, because it is half right: *sheltered from wind
means warm; flat means easy; low and enclosed means cozy.* The wind part is
true. Everything built on it is wrong.

Wind is not what makes a site cold at night. On clear, calm nights the
ground radiates its heat to the sky; the air touching it chills, grows
dense, and starts to flow downhill — slowly, silently, following the same
creases rainwater follows. And it pools where water would pool: in closed
hollows, behind terrain lips, against anything that dams it. Your sheltered
clearing is the bottom of a cold lake that refills every clear night. The
flatness that makes the foundation cheap is often old sediment — the hollow
collects water too, which is why it is flat. The shelter is real for wind
and false for cold, and cold is the one that runs your winter.

Numbers, not mood. On the worked terrain's open weather stations, the record
low is −27.6 °C. In the karst hollows nearby, cold-air pools have taken it
to −32.5 °C. Five degrees is not a nuance — it is extra firewood every
winter of the house's life, a deeper frost line under the foundation, and a
colder wall for indoor vapor to find. The clearing does not tell you this.
The land does, if you learn its language — and that language is this
chapter.

## The promise

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

## The core

### The cold-air river

Give the physics its name: **the cold-air river**. Chilled, dense air
behaves like water in slow motion — it flows downslope along the terrain's
creases, gathers into streams in the draws, and pools behind any dam. A
frost pocket is a parked river.

Three behaviors do most of the explaining. First, it flows at a walking
pace on clear calm nights and stops when wind stirs the layers — so the
stillest, prettiest nights are the coldest. Second, it dams easily: a
terrain lip, a dense hedge, a solid fence, or your own house placed across
the flow will back cold air up like a weir. Third, it drains and fills on a
schedule: pools deepen through the night and burn off after sunrise —
east-facing ground gets its reprieve first.

Now connect it to chapter 1 — and try to answer before reading on: which
corner of **the mold triangle** does a frost-pocket site push on, and how?
Recall it, then check: the moisture corner. A colder site means colder wall
and window surfaces for longer, and indoor vapor condenses on cold surfaces —
the pool outside pressures the triangle inside. Siting above the pool is
moisture management before a single material is chosen, which makes it the
cheapest move the Gaia bargain will ever get.

The design responses are short: build on a bench or shoulder above the
pool's reach, never in the hollow; keep the downhill side of your footprint
open so the river passes; and do not build your own dam — a house, garage,
or solid fence across the flow creates a private frost pocket on the uphill
side.

### The scale of permanence

P. A. Yeomans, the Australian farm designer, ranked a property's elements by
how hard they are to change — **the scale of permanence**, and the credit is
his: climate, then landform, then water, then roads and access, then trees,
then permanent buildings, then fences, then soil. Read the list once more
and notice where the house sits: sixth. Below the road.

The order is the method. Everything above the house on the list is
something you *read*, not something you fix later: you will not change the
climate, you will not move the hill, water goes where it goes, and a badly
placed road is a mistake you drive twice a day forever. Choose the house
spot first — the hollow's whole seduction — and you force every more
permanent element to accommodate the least permanent decision on the list.
That is backwards, and the land collects the interest.

Practically: your site sheet is drawn in Yeomans' order. Climate facts
first, landform second, water third, access fourth, trees fifth — and only
then candidate footprints, placed where the first five agree.

### Sun on a north slope

Winter sun is low. At the worked terrain's latitude it stands only about 20°
above the horizon at noon on the shortest days — and on a 17° slope facing
north, that light arrives at a grazing angle, nearly parallel to the ground.
A north slope does not get less sun as a figure of speech; in December it
gets almost none as a fact.

Two consequences. First, aspect is destiny on sloped land: the east-facing
component of the worked terrain means mornings are the gift — frost burns
off early where the eastern sky is open, and the house should be placed to
take it. Second, trees rule the winter: with the sun at 20°, a tree's shadow
runs nearly three times its height. The worked terrain's canopy is 15–25 m —
a single 20 m spruce south of your footprint lays a 55–60 m shadow across
it. A clearing scouted in July can be a shadow pit in December.

So scout for winter sky, not summer light: stand on the candidate spot,
face south, and read the horizon — every tree and ridge that rises more
than about two fists above it at arm's length owns your winter noon.

### Water, access, sectors

Water first, and a retrieval pull from chapter 1 — why does a wet site
matter to a wooden house even before foundations exist? Say it, then check:
**wood follows water** — every wooden surface tracks the moisture around
it, so a site that never dries is a house that never stops moving and never
stops being tested. The worked terrain gets 1,450–1,500 mm a year with no
dry season; every crease runs all year, and clay holds water at the surface
while the karst below swallows it in secret. Walk the land in rain and mark
what you see: every flow, every soggy patch, every place moss wins.

Access next — on the scale of permanence the road outranks the house. It is
the most expensive line you will draw and the hardest to move, and winter
audits it: with snow lying about 63 days and up to a meter deep, a steep
final hundred meters is not a driveway, it is a January problem. Walk the
way in and ask what it looks like under a meter of snow.

Then the sectors: around any candidate footprint, sketch a wheel of what
arrives from outside. Winter-sun sector — where the low sun actually comes
from. Cold-air sector — uphill, where the river arrives. Wind, water flows,
and the wildlife sector: the worked terrain is brown-bear country, so where
food, compost, and bins live is a siting question, not a household detail.
Zones follow naturally — what you touch daily wants to be nearest the door;
what you visit monthly can live at the edge.

## Worked example

Imagine the walk. You start at the bottom of the worked terrain, in the
hollow the misconception would buy: flat, still, lovely — and the coldest
ground on the parcel, shoreline of the −32.5 °C pool, with two wet creases
feeding it. You climb the 17° slope, north and east under a 15–25 m canopy,
past overstood hazel — leggy, light-starved coppice that is itself a
reading: light does not reach here. At about 500 m the slope breaks into a
shoulder, and the land changes voice: relict terraces, and on them the
rectangles of old building footprints.

Read the shoulder in Yeomans' order. Climate: given — 8.7 °C mean, 117
frost days, records to −27.6 °C, the pool below. Landform: a bench above
the pool's reach, with the downhill side open — the river passes instead of
parking. Water: the creases run either side of the bench, not through it,
and the terraces were built to hold soil and shed flow — someone managed
this water for generations. Access: the old lane follows the terrace line
on a kind grade — the ancestors' road agrees with the scale of permanence.
Trees: the eastern sky above the terraces is open; morning sun reaches the
bench and burns the frost off early. And only then, structures: the old
footprints sit exactly where the first five readings point. They are not
nostalgia — they are data, generations of trial and error run on this exact
slope, and every survivor stands on the bench.

One more thing about those footprints, and file it where you will trip on
it, because the answer matters to everything you stack on top: every one of
them stands on stone. Knee-high and more of it. The old builders put stone
under every house here, in a country made of trees — why stone, and why
that much of it? Chapter 4 answers.

The site sheet from this walk is one page: a north arrow; the slope drawn
with its creases; cold-air arrows down the draws and the pool's shoreline
marked in the hollow; water flows in blue; the winter-sun sector opening
east; the access line along the terraces; a bear note by the forest edge;
and two candidate footprints on the shoulder, each with one sentence for it
and one against.

## Practice

On the land, away from the screen — 40–60 minutes, and the worse the
weather, the better the data. Use your own land, a candidate parcel, or any
sloped ground you can walk (the skill transfers; the sheet still goes in
the folder).

1. Sketch a base map by hand: outline, north arrow, the slope's direction,
   main terrain creases. Ugly is fine; legible is required.
2. Go at dawn if you can — after a clear, calm night. Mark where frost or
   mist lies. That is the cold-air river caught in the act: arrows down the
   draws, a shoreline around every pool.
3. Mark winter sun: stand at each promising spot, face south, and log what
   rises more than two fists above the horizon — trees, ridge, buildings.
   Remember a tree's December shadow is nearly three times its height.
4. Walk the water: every flow line, ditch, soggy patch, moss field. If it
   is raining, follow moving water and thank the weather.
5. Walk the access: the full way in, gate to footprint. Note the steepest
   pitch and what it becomes under snow or heavy rain.
6. Around your best spot, draw the sector wheel: winter sun, cold air
   (uphill), wind, water, wildlife.
7. Mark 2–3 candidate footprints. For each, one sentence for and one
   against. File the page in the folder: this is **the site sheet**.

**How to know it worked:** a person who has never seen the land could pick
your candidate footprints off the sheet alone — and the sheet names at
least one attractive place *not* to build, with the reason.

**Short on time:** one dawn look out the window or from the gate, then a
10-minute sketch: cold-air arrows, the wettest line, the winter-sun
direction, one candidate footprint. The site sheet exists; the next walk
upgrades it.
