## The core

### The сруб's grammar: венец, паз, чашка

A сруб is not "a pile of logs" — it is a precise, ancient system, and three words carry most of it.

**Венец** — one complete ring of logs, the wall as a stack of horizontal rings. Everything about a log wall is counted in венцы: height ("six венцов to the window head"), cost, even damage ("the second венец wants replacing"). What it tells you: a wall is read course by course. Stand back and sight along the lines between венцы — even, parallel, closed lines are the signature of disciplined work; wandering gaps are a lifetime of caulking.

**Паз (longitudinal groove)** — the long groove cut into each log's underside so it saddles the log below along its whole length. Into that groove goes the packing — moss, flax, sheep's wool, by tradition and still by best practice — and the паз is where the wall keeps wind out for the next century. What it tells you: the scribe work. A wide, cleanly scribed паз that follows the lower log's actual shape closes tight as the wall settles; a narrow, hasty one leaves a wind path no packing fully cures. You cannot see the groove in a finished wall — which is exactly why you ask to see one *being* cut.

**Чашка** — the cup carved where logs cross at a corner, the joint that both carries the wall's weight and seals its coldest point. Corners are where log craft is most visible and where it fails first. What it tells you: look at the corners before anything else. Tight cups with no daylight, log ends proud past the crossing (they protect the joint), no checking split running into the cup — that is a corner to trust. Daylight in a corner is a verdict, and it is final.

### The settling years

Now the word that shapes the whole calendar of a log build. Recall **wood follows water** from chapter 1 — before reading on, answer from memory: in which direction does your billet move most, along the grain or across it?

Across. And now look at what a сруб *is*: twenty-odd logs stacked horizontally, which means twenty-odd *across-the-grain* dimensions piled one on another. As the logs dry in the wall — and they keep drying for years — every one of them shrinks in exactly the direction that is the wall's height. The wall sinks. Add the packing compressing in every паз and the cups seating deeper, and a full-height wall comes down by roughly a hand's width over about two years. This is усадка, and it is not a defect — it is the material keeping its oldest promise, at architectural scale.

**The settling years** is this chapter's named concept, and its practical law: **a сруб is finished twice.** Built once — walls up, roof on. Finished the second time — windows and doors fitted, rigid finishes applied, the chimney's collar closed — only after the settling has largely happened. What the settling years forbid meanwhile: anything rigid fixed across multiple венцы, tight-fitted frames in openings (they get sliding surrounds with a settling gap above, a piece of standard log-craft the joinery card will help you ask about), plaster too early, and any vertical post inside the building that is not fitted with an adjustable jack to be let down as the wall comes down. A carpenter who does not volunteer this list is the wrong carpenter.

And here the chapter's forward question plants itself, because you have already seen the collision coming: the walls will sink a hand's width over two years — what happens to a roof built on walls that move? Chapter 8 answers.

### The frame's grammar: post, beam, brace

The frame's vocabulary is shorter and the physics is barer. **Post** — vertical, carries load down. **Beam** — horizontal, spans between posts, carries load across. **Brace** — diagonal, and the reason any of it stands: a rectangle folds sideways under load, a triangle cannot, and the brace is what turns rectangles into triangles. No brace, no frame — just firewood standing at attention.

Notice what a frame does *not* do: settle. Its posts stand along the grain, and wood barely moves in that direction — the same anisotropy that sinks a сруб leaves a frame's height almost untouched. That single fact is now a real row on your shortlist: a frame can take its windows, doors and finishes immediately; a сруб waits out its settling years. Neither is the winner — it is a schedule and a temperament, and chapter 10 will make the worked terrain choose in the open.

**The joint is the frame's whole honesty.** In traditional work, post meets beam in a mortise-and-tenon — a tongue in a socket — locked with wooden pegs, and everything about it is *readable*: you can see the shoulder seated tight against the post, see the pegs, see whether they were offset to draw the joint closed. Modern metal plates and screws are legitimate engineering and pass codes all day — but they hide their work inside the wood, and their honesty is on the certificate, not in front of your eye. This course's preference for the visible joint is exactly that: a preference for work you can personally verify, stated as such.

### Drying, and what your billet has been quietly proving

All of this — усадка, tight cups, seated shoulders — turns on one process: wood drying toward balance with the air around it. Wood does not dry to "dry"; it dries to *equilibrium* — the moisture level where it stops exchanging with its surroundings — and then it sits there, breathing a little with the seasons. Green wood built into a wall reaches that balance in the wall, moving as it goes (the сруб way, designed around the movement). Pre-dried timber reaches it in the stack, before it is cut into joints (the frame way, which is why frame carpenters obsess about where the timber dried and how long).

You have been running this exact experiment since chapter 1. The billet on your shelf has been walking toward equilibrium with your room, one weigh-in at a time — and the mid-arc check in this chapter's practice will ask it the only question that matters: where does the curve flatten?

### The joinery card

The chapter's instrument, and the dossier's next sheet — the grammar in one table you can carry to a yard, a quote, or an interview:

| Word | In English | What it is | What it tells you about the work |
|---|---|---|---|
| «сруб» | stacked-log house | walls of horizontal logs | the settling calendar governs everything — ask who manages it |
| «венец» | crown / course | one full ring of logs | sight along the course lines: even and closed = discipline |
| «паз» | longitudinal groove | the long saddle cut, packed with moss or wool | scribe quality = wind-tightness for decades; ask to watch one cut |
| «чашка» | corner notch / cup | the corner joint, load and seal in one | look here first — daylight in a corner is a verdict |
| «усадка» | settling | the wall sinking as its logs dry | what is finished now vs in two years; jacks under every post |
| post · beam · brace | — | the frame's three words | no brace, no frame — count the triangles |
| mortise-and-tenon, pegged | — | the frame joint your eye can audit | tight shoulders, drawn pegs = honesty you can see |

## Worked example

Two scenes, both on paper.

**At the yard.** Imagine a carpenter's yard an hour from the worked terrain — a сруб for another client stands half-built, eight венцов high. You walk it with the card. Corners first: the cups are tight, the log ends stand proud past the crossings, and one corner shows a filled repair — you ask about it, and the answer ("that log checked into the cup, we replaced it") is worth more than the flaw. Then the lines: crouch and sight along the венцы — parallel, closing, good. Then the ask: *"Could I watch a паз being scribed?"* Ten minutes later you know more about this crew than any reference could tell you. Three questions finish the interview, and they are the ones the card was built for: **What moisture is this timber at, and when was it felled? How do you handle усадка over the openings? Show me a corner you cut this month.** None of these are gotchas; a good carpenter warms up answering them, and an evasive answer to any one of them is your cue to keep looking.

**At the scale.** Now the billet — the mid-arc check. Suppose your log reads like this (yours will differ; the shape will not): weighed at 1,840 g in chapter 1, then 1,758 g, 1,714 g, 1,691 g, and this week 1,679 g; width across the grain 96.0 mm at the start, 94.2 mm now. Look at the drops: 82, then 44, 23, 12. The curve is flattening — the billet is approaching equilibrium with your room, exactly as the concept above predicts. The check's discipline is to *commit*: extrapolate the flattening and write the prediction in ink — final weight, final width, and the month it gets there. "About 1,660 g, about 93.8 mm, by November" is a fine prediction. Being wrong on paper in chapter 10 will teach you more than being vague ever could.

## Practice

The joint, the card, the prediction — 45–60 minutes at a bench, a step, or the floor. Away from the screen; this one has sawdust in it.

1. Find two offcuts of softwood, roughly wrist-thick. Scrap quality is fine — this joint's fate is to be a lesson, not furniture.
2. Mark a **half-lap**: lay one piece across the other and knife or pencil its width onto both; mark half the thickness on each piece's side. Where the two laps meet, each piece gives up half — that is the whole idea.
3. Saw the shoulder cuts down to the half-thickness line on each piece.
4. Split or chisel out the waste between the cuts, then pare the beds flat. Flat matters more than pretty.
5. Fit the pieces together and *name what you feel*, out loud: rocks (a high spot in a bed), slips (over-pared, too loose), or seats with a firm push (right). This feeling in your hands is what every "tight joint" sentence in this course has been pointing at.
6. Copy **the joinery card** into the folder — and rewrite the last column in your own words. The card is the dossier piece; your words are what make it yours.
7. The wood log: re-weigh the billet, re-measure across the grain, and write the mid-arc entry — today's numbers, then the prediction in ink: final weight, final width, and when. Chapter 10 will hold you to it, kindly.

**How to know it worked:** lift the joined offcuts by one end and the other comes along; the card sits in the folder in your own words; and the wood log carries a dated prediction you can be wrong about.

**Short on time:** 10–15 minutes — skip the carving today, write the joinery card and do the re-weigh plus the prediction. The card is the sheet the dossier needs; the sawdust is the education, and it will wait for the weekend without spoiling.
